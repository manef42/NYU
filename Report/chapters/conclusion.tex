\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{chap:conclusion}

This study asked whether supervised models trained on a heterogeneous,
literature-derived database can support spacecraft material screening for two
outcomes: ignition and flame-spread rate (FSR). The answer is qualified but
useful. The database contains enough structure for strong interpolation within
experimental campaigns, yet substantially less evidence for transfer to an
unseen source. The unit held out during evaluation therefore changes the
scientific meaning of the reported performance.

This conclusion rests on 4,487 ignition observations from 87 canonical papers
and 2,531 FSR observations from 66 papers. With row-level holdouts, the selected
XGBoost ignition model achieved ROC--AUC \(0.908\pm0.010\), and the selected
random-forest FSR model achieved \(R^2=0.879\pm0.018\) with RMSE
\(0.00626\pm0.00047~\si{\metre\per\second}\). When complete papers were
withheld, ignition ROC--AUC decreased to \(0.718\pm0.044\), while FSR
performance decreased to \(R^2=0.458\pm0.222\) and RMSE increased to
\(0.01221\pm0.00412~\si{\metre\per\second}\). The consistency of this gap
across both targets is more consequential than the comparatively small
differences among the leading tree-based candidates.

The held-paper diagnostics also define the models' present limits. Ignition
probabilities are less well calibrated on unseen papers, so a threshold chosen
from known campaigns cannot be treated as a universal operating point. The FSR
regressor systematically underpredicts the sparse high-spread tail, which is a
non-conservative failure mode for fire-safety use. Feature-attribution analyses
repeatedly identify oxygen, flow, and thermal-transport variables, but these are
predictive associations in an observational database rather than causal laws.
Together, these findings rule out using either model as stand-alone
qualification or certification evidence.

The appropriate near-term role is decision support. Predictions can rank
conditions for review, expose weakly covered combinations of atmosphere,
material, geometry, flow, and gravity, and help allocate scarce reduced-gravity
tests. A useful screening output should therefore accompany every prediction
with its evaluation protocol, domain-coverage information, and an uncertainty
or out-of-distribution warning. Where coverage is poor---notably in sparse
spacecraft-relevant oxygen--pressure combinations or the high-FSR tail---the
model should trigger an experiment rather than substitute for one.

Improving transfer requires better evidence, not merely a more complex learner.
The next database release should represent rig, facility, diagnostic,
specimen-preparation, and data-reduction metadata through the developing
ontology; target experiments that fill joint coverage gaps; and evaluate models
with paper-, rig-, and facility-level holdouts. For FSR, future models should
also enforce non-negativity and test regime-appropriate physical constraints for
opposed and concurrent spread, while retaining the same source-aware validation.
For ignition, probability calibration and operating thresholds should be
validated prospectively on new campaigns.

The principal outcome of this work is thus a reproducible boundary between what
the current literature can support and what still requires measurement.
Machine learning can organise the assembled microgravity flammability evidence
and make experimental planning more systematic. It should guide expert
judgement and test prioritisation, not replace qualification testing or a
spacecraft-level fire-safety case.
