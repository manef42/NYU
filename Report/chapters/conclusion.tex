\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{chap:conclusion}

This work investigated whether a heterogeneous literature-derived database can
support machine-learning prediction of ignition and flame-spread rate for
spacecraft fire-safety applications. The answer is conditional. The database
contains useful predictive signal, but the strength of that signal depends on
the evaluation question. Models perform well when predicting new rows from
experimental campaigns already represented in training, but their performance
drops substantially when complete source papers are withheld.

The research developed two separate pipelines. The ignition pipeline used 4,487
deduplicated labelled observations from 87 canonical papers. The FSR pipeline
used 2,531 observations with numeric flame-spread-rate values from 66 canonical
papers. Both pipelines used frozen outer splits, nested hyperparameter search,
training-fold-only preprocessing, source-aware evaluation, and persisted
prediction records. This design separates model selection from final testing
and makes each reported result traceable to its source observations and split
assignments.

For ignition, the paper-square-root-weighted XGBoost candidate was selected for
row-level interpolation. It achieved ROC--AUC
\(=0.908\pm0.010\) and PR--AUC \(=0.969\pm0.004\). For paper-grouped
extrapolation, the unweighted XGBoost baseline was selected and achieved
ROC--AUC \(=0.718\pm0.044\), PR--AUC \(=0.877\pm0.030\), and Brier score
\(=0.179\pm0.017\). XGBoost was therefore the strongest ignition family in
the evaluated portfolio, but its grouped performance does not justify treating
a predicted probability as a universal fire-safety decision.

For FSR, the random-forest baseline was selected under both protocols. Under
row-level interpolation, it achieved
\(R^2=0.879\pm0.018\),
RMSE \(=0.00626\pm0.00047~\si{\metre\per\second}\), and
MAE \(=0.00210\pm0.00017~\si{\metre\per\second}\). When complete papers were
withheld, performance decreased to
\(R^2=0.458\pm0.222\),
RMSE \(=0.01221\pm0.00412~\si{\metre\per\second}\), and
MAE \(=0.00482\pm0.00114~\si{\metre\per\second}\). XGBoost remained
competitive for FSR, but the random-forest baseline provided the preferred
accuracy--stability trade-off within the persisted candidate pool.

The difference between interpolation and extrapolation is the central result.
Random row splits can place nearby observations from the same paper, rig, or
campaign on both sides of the evaluation boundary. Paper-grouped splits remove
that direct source overlap and test transfer to an unseen publication. The
resulting decline in ignition and FSR performance shows that experimental-source
information remains partly encoded in the database through rig, protocol,
material-preparation, measurement, and reporting characteristics that are not
fully represented by the current physical feature set.

This finding has two practical consequences. First, threshold operating points
for ignition cannot be transferred automatically from known campaigns to a new
source. A high-recall threshold may be appropriate for a conservative alarm,
but paper-grouped evidence shows that it can also produce an almost-always-
positive rule. Threshold selection must therefore remain an explicit safety
decision based on the relative cost of a missed ignition and a false alarm.
Second, a numerical prediction should be treated as screening evidence rather
than qualification evidence. It can rank candidate tests and identify regions
requiring attention, but it cannot replace testing, engineering judgement, or
certification.

The database is also useful as an experimental roadmap. Oxygen--pressure
coverage shows that some spacecraft-relevant conditions remain sparse,
including oxygen concentrations just above the terrestrial value and pressures
just below standard atmospheric pressure. These regions should be prioritised
for future experiments, especially when combined with representative materials,
geometries, flow directions, and gravity conditions. The objective is not
simply to increase the number of rows, but to add measurements that reduce
important gaps in the joint experimental design space.

A more complete ontology is essential for the next stage of this work.
Experimental-rig, facility, diagnostic, specimen-preparation, protocol, and
data-reduction metadata must be represented consistently so that physical
conditions can be distinguished from source fingerprints. The ontology work
initiated with Prof.~Guibaud and collaborators at ZARM in Bremen provides a
path toward this richer representation. It could support more rigorous
facility-held-out or rig-class-held-out validation in future databases.

The principal modelling extension is to introduce physical constraints for FSR
regression. The present models use physics-related features but are not required
to satisfy a flame-spread relation. For counterflow, or opposed flow, thermally
thin and thermally thick materials follow different governing FSR relations.
Future work should use a physically justified discriminator, supported by the
\texttt{thermal\_diffusion\_time} feature, to identify the applicable thermal
regime and impose the corresponding relation as a physical loss. A comparable
equation is still needed for concurrent-flow, or co-flow, configurations in
which the gas flow and flame spread travel in the same direction.

The final conclusion is therefore bounded but useful. Literature-scale machine
learning can organise microgravity flammability evidence and provide meaningful
screening support for ignition and FSR. However, the principal challenge is not
finding a higher score on mixed random rows. It is improving transfer to new
experimental sources through better ontology and metadata, targeted data
collection, source-aware validation, threshold-aware deployment, and
physically constrained FSR models. Until those steps are completed, the system
should guide experiments and support expert judgement rather than replace them.
