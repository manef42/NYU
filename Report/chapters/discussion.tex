\chapter{Discussion}
\label{chap:discussion}

The central result of this study is not that one algorithm ``solves'' material fire safety, but that the apparent predictive difficulty depends strongly on what is held out. When rows were divided at random, experiments from the same publication could occur in both training and test sets. Under that interpolation question, the flame-spread-rate (FSR) random-forest champion reached \(R^2=0.879\pm0.018\) with an RMSE of \(0.00626\pm0.00047~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), while the ignition XGBoost champion reached a ROC--AUC of \(0.908\pm0.010\). When complete papers were held out, the corresponding results fell to \(R^2=0.458\pm0.222\) and RMSE \(0.01221\pm0.00412~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) for FSR, and ROC--AUC \(0.718\pm0.044\) for ignition. The FSR error approximately doubled, and the ignition ROC--AUC decreased by about \(0.19\). This consistent row-versus-paper generalization collapse is the most consequential scientific finding. It indicates that models can interpolate effectively among conditions resembling a campaign already represented in training, but only partially transfer to an unseen publication, apparatus, laboratory practice, or sampled regime.

This chapter interprets that finding under four deliberately visible allocations: limitations (approximately 30\%), comparison with the literature (approximately 30\%), real-world applications (approximately 30\%), and future work (approximately 10\%). These proportions reflect the practical importance of defining what the models do not establish, placing their scores beside protocol-compatible evidence, and translating them into a cautious decision-support role.

\section{Limitations (approximately 30\%)}
\label{sec:discussion_limitations}

\subsection{Database heterogeneity and the unit of generalization}

The source database is a synthesis of published experiments rather than a prospectively designed factorial study. The FSR analysis contains 2,531 usable target rows from 66 canonical papers; the ignition analysis contains 4,487 deduplicated rows from 87 papers, of which 3,383 report ignition and 1,104 report no ignition. These counts provide considerably more observations than a single campaign, but rows are not independent replications of a common data-generating process. A publication may contribute many nearby settings from one rig, material batch, diagnostic method, gravity platform, or laboratory. Another may contribute only a handful of observations in a distinct regime. Consequently, a row-level split can place near-neighbours---and campaign-specific signatures---on both sides of the evaluation boundary.

The grouped protocol addresses the clearest version of this dependence by constructing canonical paper identities from DOI or citation and prohibiting paper overlap. It therefore tests a harder and more relevant question: transfer to a publication that was absent from model fitting and inner hyperparameter selection. It does not, however, guarantee transfer to every future spacecraft material or operating envelope. Papers can share laboratories, investigators, facilities, wire stocks, reporting conventions, or inherited correlations. Conversely, two campaigns in one paper may differ materially. ``Paper'' is thus a defensible, reproducible proxy for experimental source, not a perfect ontological definition of domain. A still stronger validation would hold out laboratories, facilities, material families, gravity platforms, or time periods, but the current metadata do not support all such partitions consistently.

The collapse under paper holdout is evidence that publication identity carries predictive structure beyond the intended physical variables. That structure may include legitimate regime information omitted from the schema, such as apparatus heat losses or material morphology, as well as source-specific measurement and reporting practices. The result should not be described simply as overfitting in the narrow algorithmic sense. It is more accurately a combination of source shift, incomplete covariate description, clustered sampling, and possible outcome-definition differences. The large FSR fold variability---\(R^2\) ranging from approximately \(0.018\) to \(0.746\) for the grouped champion---further shows that transfer difficulty depends on which papers are withheld.

\subsection{Missingness, measurement comparability, and representation}

Literature synthesis inevitably produces sparse and uneven feature coverage. Material thermophysical properties may be tabulated, inferred from material labels, or absent. Geometry can be reported in formats that are not directly commensurate across wires, flat samples, cylinders, and spheres. Pressure, flow, oxygen concentration, ignition settings, and gravity are not varied independently, and some are fixed or omitted within individual campaigns. The preprocessing contract handles this computationally: numeric values are median-imputed for most families, categorical missingness is assigned an ``Unknown'' level, and XGBoost can use native numeric missingness. Computational handling is not equivalent to recovering the unobserved physics.

Missingness may itself be source-dependent. If one laboratory systematically reports a property and another does not, a tree can use the missingness pattern as a source fingerprint. This can improve random-split prediction while harming transfer. Median imputation also compresses genuinely different unknown cases to a common value, while a constant categorical level conflates ``not measured'', ``not applicable'', and ``not reported''. The grouped results therefore integrate both the difficulty of physical extrapolation and the consequences of imperfect harmonisation.

Feature engineering improves consistency but does not remove these concerns. The ignition pipeline uses 32 numeric and five categorical features, including material, gas-transport, operating-condition, geometry, flow-direction, gravity-regime, and ignition-method descriptors. The FSR pipeline detects the available numeric and categorical roles after excluding explicit source identifiers, duplicate target fields, and post-outcome variables. Derived quantities such as Reynolds, Peclet, Prandtl, volumetric heat capacity, thermal diffusivity, and thermal diffusion time can encode useful physical structure. Yet derived features share inputs and can be mutually dependent; their values are only as reliable as the source measurements and assumptions used to calculate them.

One schema discrepancy requires explicit qualification: the persisted FSR feature
manifest includes categorical \texttt{Ignition}, although the pipeline
documentation describes ignition fields as excluded. Its reported tree and SHAP
importance is zero, but that does not substitute for a controlled ablation.
Future FSR evaluation should remove the field and regenerate frozen comparisons
before asserting that the documented and executed schemas are identical.

\subsection{Imbalance and observational sampling}

Two forms of imbalance matter. First, ignition is a majority-positive task: approximately three quarters of the 4,487 records are ignition cases. A high PR--AUC is therefore partly facilitated by prevalence and must be read beside ROC--AUC, Brier score, sensitivity, specificity, and threshold-dependent performance. The four stored threshold policies make the trade-off explicit. For example, the grouped champion's F1-oriented policy attains very high aggregate sensitivity but very low specificity, whereas the balanced-accuracy policy gives much more even sensitivity and specificity. No threshold is universally preferable; its acceptability depends on the consequences of false negatives and false positives.

Second, papers contribute unequal row counts. A densely sampled campaign can dominate empirical risk even if it represents a narrow region. The candidate portfolios test no, square-root, and inverse paper weights, and weight-blind families use deterministic probability-proportional resampling. These mechanisms change each source's influence, but cannot manufacture missing regimes. The selected grouped champions were the unweighted baselines: random forest for FSR and XGBoost for ignition. This does not prove that source imbalance is unimportant. It only shows that the tested weighting corrections did not improve the selection objective enough under these frozen splits.

Observational coverage creates a more fundamental limitation. Oxygen, pressure, gravity, flow, geometry, material properties, and ignition practice were chosen by investigators for particular scientific questions. Their joint distribution is therefore neither random nor uniform. For example, a material may occur mainly in one geometry and gravity regime, while a pressure range may be associated with one facility. A flexible learner can predict from these correlations without separating their physical effects. Sparse partial-gravity observations are especially problematic because lunar and Martian gravity conditions cannot be treated as densely sampled interpolation domains merely because ``gravity'' is a numeric feature.

\subsection{Model dependence, metrics, and uncertainty}

Model rankings are contingent on the tested candidates, search spaces, preprocessing, loss functions, seeds, and selection policy. The configuration files declare 34 FSR and 26 ignition candidates, but the persisted merged FSR manifest contains 25 candidates and the formal ignition selection record omits the available MLP shards. The selected FSR champion was random forest rather than XGBoost, while ignition selected XGBoost. These results apply to the persisted comparison pools; they neither establish complete execution of every configured recipe nor prove that the selected model is optimal among all possible algorithms.

Likewise, a point estimate is not a complete account of performance. \(R^2\) is sensitive to the target variance in a held-out fold and can be unstable when a small set of papers defines a narrow or shifted range. FSR MAPE is extremely large because percentage error is ill-conditioned when observed spread approaches zero; RMSE and MAE in physical units are more interpretable here. For ignition, ROC--AUC measures ranking across thresholds but not probability calibration or a mission-specific operating point. The paper-cluster bootstrap, repeated splits, per-paper metrics, calibration outputs, and fold standard deviations should therefore accompany any headline number.

The final refitted artifacts also have a different evidential status from the outer-fold results. Refitting uses all labelled rows and is appropriate for producing a deployable model, but its training fit is not an unbiased estimate of future performance. Only the nested, untouched outer-fold predictions support comparative claims. The model cards correctly preserve this distinction.

\subsection{SHAP and the boundary between explanation and causation}

The explainability outputs describe model behaviour, not causal mechanisms. For ignition, the global XGBoost SHAP ranking assigns high mean absolute contributions to oxygen, absolute flow speed, Prandtl number, gas molar mass, fuel pyrolysis temperature, and ignition time. Yet grouped permutation importance places fuel pyrolysis temperature, ignition time, fuel heat capacity, thermal diffusion time, and a sample dimension at the top. For FSR, SHAP ranks thermal diffusion time and oxygen prominently, while held-out permutation importance ranks oxygen, flow velocity, gas thermal conductivity, and thermal diffusion time most strongly. Differences are expected because SHAP and permutation importance answer different questions and react differently to correlated inputs.

A SHAP value is an additive decomposition of a particular fitted function relative to a background distribution. It does not show that intervening on that feature would produce the attributed change. Correlated and derived variables allow credit to be redistributed among substitutes. Source confounding can make an apparatus-correlated variable appear physically decisive. Missingness patterns may attract attribution. Mean absolute SHAP also suppresses direction, interactions, and regime dependence. The physically plausible appearance of many rankings is reassuring, but plausibility is not validation of a causal law. Claims such as ``oxygen causes the predicted increase'' require intervention, mechanistic identification, or a causal design that this observational database does not provide.

The safest interpretation is predictive: within the fitted model and sampled distribution, these features are influential for separating or estimating outcomes. The permutation results add a transfer-oriented check by measuring score degradation on held-out data, but they remain model- and sample-dependent. Explainability is therefore best used to audit whether the learner relies on obviously inappropriate signals, to generate hypotheses, and to identify data-collection priorities. It should not replace combustion theory or controlled experimentation.

\section{Comparison with the Literature (approximately 30\%)}
\label{sec:discussion_literature}

\subsection{Rivera/Jose wire-specific machine learning}

Rivera, Jose, and colleagues report a literature-derived electrical-wire FSR study in which categorical regime descriptors and augmentation improve random ten-fold cross-validation performance, with several nonlinear models exceeding \(R^2=0.9\) \cite{riveraWireML}. Their database contains approximately 1,200 wire observations and explicitly represents core and insulation materials, spread direction, and configuration. Categorisation raises the reported performance of gradient boosting, KNN, SVM, decision trees, and MLP, while bootstrap augmentation is particularly effective for gradient boosting and KNN. This supports two conclusions also visible here: flame spread is strongly nonlinear, and categorical information can expose regime structure that numeric properties alone may not capture.

The numerical results must not be presented as if they were generated by equivalent protocols. The Rivera/Jose work uses random cross-validation and explicitly characterises its result as robustness within the compiled database rather than strict leave-source-out transfer. Its scope is electrical wires, and its target is reported in \(\mathrm{mm\,s^{-1}}\). The present FSR database is broader in geometry and source coverage, reports the model target in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), and makes paper-disjoint nested evaluation the primary scientific test. Hyperparameter search, feature definitions, data versions, augmentation, and source composition also differ. A direct statement that \(0.458\) is ``worse than'' \(>0.9\) would confound task and protocol.

The fair comparison is question-specific. On row-level interpolation, this study's random-forest \(R^2=0.879\pm0.018\) is close to, though generally below, the strongest wire-specific random-CV values. On paper-level extrapolation, the same family falls to \(R^2=0.458\pm0.222\). Rivera/Jose do not report an equivalent paper-disjoint score, so their \(>0.9\) result neither contradicts nor establishes performance on this harder transfer task. Instead, their stated caveat and the present protocol gap jointly reinforce the need to name the validation unit whenever quoting accuracy.

There is also a substantive difference in what categorisation can encode. Wire-specific labels such as insulation chemistry, core material, concurrent/opposed spread, and orientation can partition physically distinct regimes. In a broader database, adding similarly detailed categories may improve prediction, but it may also increase sparsity and source fingerprinting. The present models exclude explicit paper identifiers and post-outcome features, yet legitimate categories can remain highly campaign-specific. Their value should therefore be tested under grouped validation, not assumed from random-CV gains.

The augmentation comparison is similarly instructive. Rivera/Jose find bootstrap resampling particularly useful, while noise injection and GAN augmentation can create statistically plausible but not necessarily physically consistent combinations. The present FSR benchmark treats bootstrap copies as reweighting observed conditions; copies inherit the source paper and are never added to the test set. Its selected FSR champions use the real-data condition rather than bootstrap augmentation. Thus, augmentation did not compensate for source shift in the selected model. This supports a conservative principle: resampling can alter optimisation and density, but it cannot supply a new material, apparatus, or gravity regime.

\subsection{XGBoost and the tabular-learning literature}

The strength of tree ensembles is consistent with broader tabular-learning evidence. Shwartz-Ziv and Armon compare XGBoost with several deep tabular architectures on eleven datasets and find that XGBoost generally outperforms individual deep models, requires less tuning, and is less sensitive to whether a dataset appeared in the proposing model's original paper \cite{shwartzZiv2022tabular}. The present database has precisely the characteristics that often favour tree ensembles: mixed numeric and categorical predictors, nonlinear interactions, sparse cells, missing fields, unequal scales, and modest sample size relative to the variety of regimes.

The ignition outcome fits this expectation strongly. XGBoost occupies the top of both interpolation and grouped leaderboards; the paper-square-root variant is selected for interpolation and the unweighted baseline for extrapolation. By comparison, decision tree, KNN, and SVM candidates generally rank lower, while the tested focal XGBoost variants perform near chance. This last result is a reminder that a generally strong architecture does not rescue an unsuitable objective implementation or weighting combination.

The FSR result is more nuanced. Random forest narrowly leads interpolation and is selected for grouped transfer, while several XGBoost variants remain competitive. The selected random forest uses no paper weighting, no augmentation, and standard identity-target regression. Its grouped \(R^2\) is \(0.458\), compared with \(0.441\) for the strongest listed augmented paper-weighted XGBoost candidate. This difference is small relative to fold variation and should not be elevated into a universal claim that bagging is superior to boosting. It shows only that, within the tested portfolio and policy, the random-forest baseline offered the preferred accuracy--stability combination.

The MLP candidates do not outperform the tree ensembles, particularly under grouped FSR evaluation, where their mean \(R^2\) values can become negative and highly variable. This agrees directionally with the tabular literature, but the explanation is not that neural networks are intrinsically inappropriate. Data volume, regularisation, architecture, optimisation, and representation all matter. Shwartz-Ziv and Armon also find that ensembles combining deep models with XGBoost can outperform either component alone \cite{shwartzZiv2022tabular}. The present work did not evaluate a formally stacked, outer-fold-safe heterogeneous ensemble, so it cannot rule out such gains.

\subsection{Physics-informed learning and combustion knowledge}

Physics-informed neural networks constrain learning with residuals of governing differential equations and boundary or initial conditions \cite{raissi2019pinn}. More broadly, physics-informed machine learning seeks to combine data with conservation laws, symmetries, constitutive knowledge, or admissibility constraints \cite{karniadakis2021physicsinformed}. This is attractive for flame spread because experimental coverage is sparse, predictions must remain physically plausible, and extrapolation is precisely where unconstrained correlations are weakest.

The present models are physics-featured rather than physics-constrained. Inputs include thermophysical properties, dimensionless groups, geometry, oxygen, pressure, flow, gravity, and ignition conditions, but the learned function is not required to satisfy mass, species, momentum, or energy balances. Optional monotonic constraints on oxygen exist for selected XGBoost configurations, yet a monotonic feature constraint is not equivalent to a combustion model. Likewise, categorising gravity or spread direction supplies domain structure but does not create a PINN.

Applying a conventional PINN directly would require an agreed governing formulation, state fields, boundary conditions, and sufficient spatial or temporal observations. This database predominantly contains tabular experimental summaries and endpoint outcomes, not common-domain field solutions. The Rivera/Jose paper also notes that the theoretical framework remains incomplete for direct physics-informed deployment \cite{riveraWireML}. Therefore, the immediate opportunity is more modest: enforce nonnegative FSR, use dimensionally consistent representations, penalise known inadmissible combinations, model regime-specific monotonicity where defensible, or hybridise reduced-order heat-transfer relations with learned residuals. Such constraints must still be evaluated on held-out sources; physical plausibility alone does not guarantee empirical transfer.

\subsection{Spacecraft fire-safety context}

Guibaud and colleagues identify fire as a major concern for inhabited exploration, particularly as missions move farther from immediate Earth support and involve different atmospheres, gravity levels, confinement, and operational constraints \cite{guibaud2022spacecraft}. In that setting, ignition and flame spread are not abstract prediction targets. They contribute to material selection, wire and component design, experiment prioritisation, contingency planning, and the evaluation of how a local ignition might develop.

The present findings add a data-governance qualification to that motivation. Historical evidence can support useful interpolation, but mission-relevant environments may resemble a new source domain rather than another row from a known campaign. Lunar and Martian partial gravity, altered oxygen and pressure, aging materials, realistic ventilation, and integrated spacecraft geometries may lie in sparsely observed combinations. The correct literature-level conclusion is therefore not that machine learning replaces fire testing, but that it can organise heterogeneous evidence and identify where that evidence is or is not transferable.

\section{Real-World Applications (approximately 30\%)}
\label{sec:discussion_applications}

\subsection{Screening and prioritisation}

The most defensible near-term application is screening. Given candidate conditions in the documented schema, the ignition artifact can rank cases by predicted ignition probability, and the FSR artifact can estimate spread rate for records sufficiently similar to the training support. This can reduce a large design space to a smaller set requiring detailed simulation or experiment. A screening tool can, for example, flag material--geometry--atmosphere combinations whose predictions and uncertainty suggest elevated concern, or prioritise boundary cases where models disagree.

Such use should be explicitly asymmetric. A high-risk prediction may justify additional investigation; a low-risk prediction should not by itself waive testing. False reassurance is particularly consequential in spacecraft fire safety. Threshold selection must therefore follow an operational loss function rather than a generic metric. If missing a potentially ignitable case is costly, a sensitivity-oriented threshold may be appropriate, accepting more false alarms. If scarce test capacity is the binding constraint, a more specific threshold may be chosen, but the resulting missed-case risk must be quantified. The repository deliberately stores MCC-, F1-, balanced-accuracy-, and Youden-\(J\)-oriented thresholds rather than declaring one universally correct.

FSR screening should rely primarily on RMSE and MAE in physical units, not MAPE near zero. Predictions should be accompanied by a domain flag and an interval or empirical error summary. The grouped champion's mean RMSE of \(0.0122~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), with substantial fold variation, is large relative to many low-spread observations. Ranking may therefore be more reliable than precise point estimation in underrepresented regimes. Where a decision depends on a narrow FSR boundary, confirmatory testing is necessary.

\subsection{Experiment design and database curation}

A second application is directing new data collection. Per-paper errors can identify campaigns that are systematically difficult to predict. Disagreement between random and grouped performance identifies fields that may encode source-specific rather than transferable information. Explainability, used cautiously, can nominate influential variables whose measurement completeness deserves improvement. For example, the repeated importance of fuel pyrolysis temperature, ignition time, thermal diffusion time, oxygen, flow, and gas properties suggests that missing or inconsistently defined values in these fields can be costly.

The tool can also support coverage analysis before an experiment. A proposed test condition can be compared with ranges, categories, and combinations represented in training. A case in a familiar oxygen--pressure range but a new material--geometry--gravity combination should be labelled out of distribution even if each marginal value is individually familiar. This distinction encourages experiments that fill joint gaps rather than merely adding dense repetitions to an already dominant campaign.

Partial-gravity testing is an especially strong candidate for prioritisation. Data collected at lunar- or Martian-relevant gravity, with controlled atmosphere and flow, would test whether a numeric gravity feature transfers smoothly or whether regime-specific dynamics dominate. Because spaceflight experiments are expensive, model uncertainty and coverage can help allocate them, but the acquisition strategy should reserve samples for prospective validation rather than using every new result for immediate retraining.

\subsection{Material and design decision support}

Within a formal engineering workflow, predictions can be one line of evidence in a multi-criteria decision analysis. Candidate materials may be evaluated on ignition propensity and expected spread alongside toxicity, smoke, mechanical performance, electrical function, mass, manufacturability, aging, and compatibility. The models can reveal trade-offs: one material may score lower for ignition under represented conditions yet have uncertain spread behaviour in a new geometry. Presenting both tasks separately is essential because ignition probability and post-ignition spread rate are related but not interchangeable safety endpoints.

A suitable report for each candidate would contain: the input record and units; missing or imputed fields; the model and data fingerprint; interpolation and grouped evidence; the selected threshold policy; predicted probability or FSR; uncertainty or fold-based error context; nearest supported regimes; out-of-distribution warnings; and known limitations. It should also record whether the recommendation is merely ``test first'', ``requires expert review'', or ``within represented screening envelope''. Raw probabilities without this provenance invite overinterpretation.

Human review remains central. A combustion specialist should assess whether the implied trend is physically credible and whether the case violates assumptions hidden by the tabular representation. A materials or systems engineer should evaluate integration effects absent from the database. A safety authority should determine the acceptable evidence and margins. Model output is an aid to these roles, not an automated disposition.

\subsection{Uncertainty, abstention, and out-of-distribution use}

The current repeated-fold standard deviations and cluster-bootstrap intervals provide evaluation uncertainty, not a complete case-specific predictive distribution. Operational use needs both aleatoric uncertainty, arising from experimental variability, and epistemic uncertainty, arising from sparse support and model uncertainty. An ensemble across seeds, folds, or model families could estimate disagreement. Conformal methods could provide empirical intervals or prediction sets if calibrated under a defensible exchangeability assumption. Neither removes distribution shift; grouped or source-conditional calibration remains necessary.

Abstention is a safety feature. The system should decline to issue a routine prediction when required fields are absent, categorical levels are unseen, the query is distant from training support, model portfolios disagree substantially, or a physically admissible range is violated. Out-of-distribution detection should consider mixed data types and joint structure. A simple min--max range check is insufficient because impossible or unsupported combinations can lie inside all marginal ranges.

For ignition, calibration must be examined under held-out papers. A nominal probability of 0.8 is operationally meaningful only if similar grouped predictions ignite at approximately that rate, within uncertainty. For FSR, an interval should be checked for coverage by paper and regime, not only pooled rows. Performance dashboards should expose the worst papers and regimes rather than average away failure pockets.

\subsection{No replacement for certification or qualification}

These artifacts are not certification models. They have not undergone prospective external validation, formal software qualification, independent verification and validation, uncertainty qualification for a specified design envelope, or acceptance by a regulatory or mission assurance authority. The database is observational and heterogeneous; source-held-out performance is moderate; and the models do not enforce governing physics. No model output should replace required material flammability tests, wire qualification, hazard analysis, engineering judgement, or certification evidence.

This limitation does not make the models unusable. Screening and decision support can be valuable precisely because they precede authoritative testing. The proper claim is that the models summarise existing evidence and can prioritise resources under documented uncertainty. The improper claim is that a predicted ``no ignition'' or low FSR establishes compliance.

Deployment would require version control and auditability. The repository already records data hashes, feature manifests, split assignments, selected hyperparameters, model cards, and out-of-fold predictions. An operational service should additionally lock the software environment, validate units and schemas, log every query and response, monitor drift, and trigger review when the database or artifact changes. Evaluation artifacts and refitted artifacts must remain clearly separated: the former support performance claims; the latter generate predictions.

\subsection{A staged deployment concept}

A cautious implementation can proceed in stages. In an offline research stage, users inspect leaderboards, paper-level errors, learning curves, calibration, and explanations without affecting decisions. In a shadow stage, predictions accompany conventional assessments but are not used to change them; discrepancies are adjudicated and logged. In a controlled screening stage, outputs may prioritise tests within a predeclared envelope, with mandatory abstention rules and expert sign-off. Only after external prospective evidence could a broader role be considered.

Success criteria should be decision-centred. A useful screener is not merely one with a high ROC--AUC; it reduces testing burden or identifies hazardous cases without exceeding a tolerated miss rate, while remaining calibrated across sources. A useful regressor does not merely minimise pooled RMSE; it provides intervals that cover new-paper observations and rankings that support experimental prioritisation. These criteria align model development with fire-safety outcomes rather than leaderboard optimisation.

\section{Future Work (approximately 10\%)}
\label{sec:discussion_future}

The immediate priority is external, prospective validation. New experiments should be registered before outcomes are observed, processed through a frozen artifact, and retained as an untouched external test set. Validation should report both pooled and source-specific performance, calibration, abstention frequency, and errors by material, geometry, atmosphere, and gravity regime. Only after this evaluation should the new records enter training.

More partial-gravity data are needed. Purposeful lunar- and Martian-gravity campaigns should vary oxygen, pressure, flow, material, and geometry sufficiently to distinguish gravity effects from source and apparatus effects. Replicate measurements and harmonised uncertainty reporting would separate experimental variability from domain shift.

Uncertainty and out-of-distribution detection should become first-class outputs. Paper-aware ensembles, grouped conformal calibration where assumptions are supportable, mixed-type distance or density models, and explicit abstention thresholds should be evaluated prospectively. Coverage should be measured by source and regime, not only globally.

The database would benefit from a shared ontology: canonical material identifiers; geometry and orientation definitions; units; uncertainty fields; missingness reasons; facility, rig, and diagnostic metadata; and controlled vocabularies for ignition and extinction outcomes. Better provenance would allow laboratory- or facility-held-out validation and reduce accidental source fingerprints.

Finally, physically constrained models should be tested without assuming that a full PINN is immediately appropriate. Useful steps include nonnegative FSR outputs, dimensionally consistent features, regime-aware monotonic constraints, conservation-inspired penalties, and hybrids that learn corrections to reduced-order flame-spread relations. Fully physics-informed formulations become credible only when governing equations, boundary conditions, and compatible field data are available \cite{raissi2019pinn,karniadakis2021physicsinformed}. Every proposed constraint must be assessed under the same paper-disjoint and prospective protocols used to test statistical models.
