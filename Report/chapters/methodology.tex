\chapter{Methodology}
\label{chap:methodology}

This chapter describes the path from published experimental evidence to
held-out machine-learning predictions. The database records individual
experimental conditions, but the relevant unit of transfer is often the source
publication: rows from one paper may share an apparatus, material batch,
diagnostic method, operating procedure, and experimental campaign. The
analysis therefore distinguishes row-level interpolation from paper-level
extrapolation, confines all adaptive choices to training data, and preserves
an audit trail from each reported score to its source rows, split assignment,
candidate configuration, and fitted model.

\section{Assembly of the microgravity flammability database}
\label{sec:database-assembly}

\subsection{Literature screening and inclusion}

The database was assembled before either machine-learning pipeline was designed.
Approximately 130 experimental papers and technical reports published from 1966
onward were screened for solid-fuel combustion under microgravity,
partial-gravity, or spacecraft-relevant atmospheric and flow conditions.
Eighty-seven publications provided sufficiently resolved information to map
ignition or flame-spread-rate (FSR) measurements to individual database rows.

A source was included for modelling only when the experiment, specimen,
environment, and reported outcome could be linked at row level. A qualitative
result could inform the literature review, but it could not be used for
supervised learning without recoverable experimental conditions.

Each retained row preserves its human-readable article citation, author
information, and DOI where available. This provenance is essential because
observations from one publication are clustered and ambiguous values must remain
traceable to the original source.

The database is not a balanced factorial design. A median paper contributes
32 rows, whereas some campaigns contribute several hundred nearby conditions
measured on one rig. This uneven structure makes a random row split optimistic:
observations from the same campaign can appear in both training and test data.
Holding out a complete paper removes a larger, internally correlated block.

The overview also explains why the analysis focuses on two supervised tasks.
Ignition labels are nearly complete, and numeric FSR is available for about half
of the assembled rows. Flame length, heat-release rate, and smoke/aerosol
measurements are too sparse to support comparable predictive models.

\subsection{Extraction strategy}

A conventional optical-character-recognition workflow was considered first but
was not suitable for this literature. The sources range from old scanned papers
to long technical memoranda. Their notation, units, tables, page layouts, and
graphical conventions vary widely. In many cases, values appear only as plotted
markers rather than machine-readable tables. Generic OCR can misread subscripts,
separate values from their headings, and associate a correct number with the
wrong experimental series.

The final workflow was AI-assisted and human-verified:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Each source was read to identify the experiment, specimen, controlled
  conditions, measured outputs, and units.

  \item An AI-assisted extraction pass recovered structured candidate values
  from text, tables, and figures.

  \item The database curator checked every retained row against the original
  source and corrected missing, misread, or incorrectly associated values.

  \item Units and category names were standardised, while the original citation
  and explanatory notes were retained for traceability.
\end{enumerate}

AI accelerated extraction but did not determine final data values. Manual
verification remained the acceptance step. Values obtained from plotted figures
were recorded only to the precision supported by marker size and axis
resolution. Ambiguous units, series assignments, or conditions were resolved
from the caption and surrounding text where possible; otherwise, the value was
left missing.

For dense graphical data, points were digitised with WebPlotDigitizer
\cite{webplotdigitizer}. Axes were calibrated from published ticks, including
explicit treatment of logarithmic axes. Each colour or marker series was traced
separately, overlapping points were checked at high magnification, and exported
coordinates were compared with axis limits and values stated in the text. This
approach was preferred to automated OCR because it preserves control over axis
calibration, series membership, and graphical precision.

\subsection{Row structure, outputs, and quality control}

The database schema groups fields into citation, specimen, flow, apparatus,
outputs, and notes. Predictor candidates describe sample geometry and
dimensions, fuel and core thermophysical properties, oxygen concentration,
diluent, gas properties, pressure, flow, gravity, and ignition conditions.
The five recorded flammability outputs are ignition, flame length, FSR,
heat-release rate, and smoke/aerosols
(\cref{tab:flammability-variables}).

The raw spreadsheet uses a category banner as its first row and field names as
its second. Values were converted to SI units whenever the source supported a
defensible conversion: length in metres, pressure in pascals, velocity in
\si{\metre\per\second}, power in watts, time in seconds, density in
\si{\kilogram\per\metre\cubed}, conductivity in
\si{\watt\per\metre\per\kelvin}, heat capacity in
\si{\joule\per\kilogram\per\kelvin}, and FSR in
\si{\metre\per\second}. Values with incompatible dimensions were not coerced
into a target field merely to increase completeness.

Ignition labels, fuel thermophysical properties, oxygen concentration, and
diluent are almost complete. Geometry, gravity, pressure, and ignition method
are also available for more than 98\% of rows. Flow velocity is less complete
at 79.5\%. Numeric FSR is available for 51.5\% of the raw database, which is
why the FSR regression cohort is smaller than the ignition cohort.

Flame length (4.3\%), heat-release rate (1.1\%), and smoke/aerosols (1.8\%)
are too sparse for comparable supervised-learning tasks. Ignition power
(18.8\%) and ignition duration (45.6\%) are retained as potentially useful
predictors, with fold-local treatment of missing values. Free-text material
names, apparatus names, facility labels, and notes are excluded from model
inputs because they can identify an experimental source without providing a
transferable physical description.

Quality control removes empty rows and columns, standardises target labels,
removes exact modelling duplicates, and records unparseable values as missing
rather than guessed. Physical-domain checks reject impossible concentrations,
non-positive pressures, and non-positive FSR values. Unit conversion is applied
before duplicate detection so that equivalent observations reported in different
units cannot remain as separate rows.

Canonical paper identity is derived from the citation record and stored in the
form \texttt{citation::<normalized-citation>}. Dataset shape, column roles,
target prevalence, paper counts, and a content fingerprint are recorded during
data validation and checked again when frozen splits are loaded.

The two modelling tasks use different valid subsets. After target
standardisation and duplicate removal, ignition classification contains 4,487
rows from 87 canonical papers: 3,383 ignition observations and 1,104
no-ignition observations. FSR regression contains 2,531 rows with a numeric
FSR target from 66 papers. The FSR papers contribute between 1 and 193 rows,
with a median of 22.5 rows per paper. Dataset fingerprints are stored with the
split and evaluation artifacts so that reported results cannot silently refer
to a changed database.

\subsection{Sampling and class imbalance}
\label{sec:method-imbalance}

The assembled literature contains two important forms of imbalance: unequal
source-paper contribution and unequal ignition-class frequency.

First, papers contribute very different numbers of observations. Some represent
one small experiment, whereas others contain many closely spaced conditions
from the same rig or campaign. As shown in \cref{fig:method-imbalance}(a), 36
of the 87 papers account for more than 80\% of all rows. A model trained by
treating every row equally can therefore be dominated by a small number of
densely sampled campaigns.

This source-size imbalance motivates three methodological choices. Paper
weighting is evaluated as a candidate modelling choice. Paper-grouped
evaluation tests transfer to a source paper not seen during fitting. For FSR
extrapolation, bootstrap uncertainty intervals resample complete paper clusters
rather than treating correlated rows as independent observations.

Second, ignition is a majority-positive classification task. In the raw
assembled database, 3,875 of 5,057 observations (76.6\%) are ignition cases,
whereas 1,182 observations (23.4\%) are no-ignition cases
(\cref{fig:method-imbalance}(b)). After cleaning, the ignition cohort contains
3,383 ignition and 1,104 no-ignition observations.

An always-ignite classifier would therefore achieve an apparently high
accuracy without distinguishing the two outcomes. Accuracy is not used as a
selection metric. Instead, the ignition pipeline compares ROC--AUC, PR--AUC,
and Brier score, and it reports threshold-dependent sensitivity, specificity,
precision, F1 score, balanced accuracy, and MCC. Class weighting and paper
weighting are tested as candidate design choices rather than assumed to correct
these imbalances automatically.

\section{Methods common to both machine-learning pipelines}
\label{sec:common-pipeline}

The ignition and FSR pipelines share the same evaluation principles while
addressing different prediction tasks. The ignition pipeline estimates the
probability of ignition,
\(\widehat p=P(y_{\mathrm{ign}}=1\mid\mathbf{x})\). The FSR pipeline estimates
a continuous flame-spread rate, \(\widehat v_{\mathrm{FSR}}\). In both cases,
model comparison is based on frozen outer splits, training-fold-only
preprocessing, nested hyperparameter selection, and separate interpolation and
extrapolation results.

Repeated splits estimate performance within the empirical mixture of papers and
conditions represented in the database. They are not an external validation
set. In particular, paper-grouped extrapolation is stronger evidence of
transfer than a random row split, but it does not guarantee performance for a
future spacecraft configuration outside the represented experimental envelope.

\subsection{Benchmarking motivation and model families}

The closest prior machine-learning study of flame spread is Rivera, Jose, and
colleagues' electrical-wire database study \cite{riveraWireML}. They compiled
approximately 1,200 observations and compared linear regression, elastic net,
stochastic gradient descent, Bayesian ridge, kernel ridge, decision trees,
\(k\)-nearest neighbours, gradient boosting, support-vector regression, and
multilayer perceptrons under random ten-fold cross-validation. Nonlinear
families were competitive, whereas the linear and shrinkage models were less
useful for their heterogeneous wire dataset.

The present work retains the families that address distinct modelling
hypotheses, upgrades gradient boosting to XGBoost, and adds random forest as a
bagging control for the FSR task. Linear regression, elastic net, SGD, Bayesian
ridge, and kernel ridge are not repeated because they would expand the
portfolio without testing a new scientific question.

The model families have the following roles:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{XGBoost} is the primary gradient-boosted tree model. It is a
  strong baseline for heterogeneous tabular data with nonlinear interactions,
  mixed feature types, missing values, and unequal scales
  \cite{shwartzZiv2022tabular,chenGuestrin2016}.

  \item \textbf{Random forest} is included for FSR as a bagging-based
  comparison with boosting.

  \item \textbf{Decision trees} provide a transparent nonlinear baseline.

  \item \textbf{\(k\)-nearest neighbours} test whether similar experimental
  conditions have similar outcomes in the engineered feature space.

  \item \textbf{SVM/SVR} compare linear and radial decision boundaries or
  regression surfaces.

  \item \textbf{MLPs} test whether a smooth neural approximation improves
  performance under the same leakage-controlled evaluation procedure.
\end{itemize}

These family choices are motivated by prior work, not assumed to determine the
result. All candidates are evaluated under the same frozen splits and selection
rules.

\subsection{Features, leakage exclusions, and preprocessing}

The pipelines use measurements available before the outcome is known. The
ignition pipeline predicts whether ignition occurs; the FSR pipeline predicts
the measured flame-spread rate. Each task therefore uses a task-specific valid
subset of the database and a corresponding feature manifest.

Predictors describe the specimen, gas, operating environment, and ignition
conditions. They include:

\begin{itemize}
  \item sample geometry and characteristic dimensions;
  \item fuel and core density, conductivity, heat capacity, pyrolysis
  temperature, thermal diffusivity, and volumetric heat capacity;
  \item oxygen concentration, diluent, gas properties, and dimensionless
  transport quantities;
  \item pressure, flow velocity, gravity, ignition method, ignition power, and
  ignition duration where available;
  \item derived quantities, including Reynolds number, Peclet number, Prandtl
  number, and thermal diffusion time.
\end{itemize}

The ignition pipeline uses a fixed manifest of 32 numeric and five categorical
features. The FSR pipeline detects the applicable numeric and categorical
fields from the cleaned schema and excludes fields that are unavailable or
inappropriate for FSR prediction.

The following fields are excluded from both pipelines:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Citation fields, DOI, authors, paper identifiers, facility names, rig
  names, internal apparatus descriptions, free-text material labels, and notes.
  These can reveal the experimental source without providing a transferable
  physical description.

  \item Flame length, heat-release rate, smoke/aerosols, extinction indicators,
  duplicate target columns, and other post-outcome quantities.

  \item The ignition label when predicting FSR, and the numeric FSR target when
  predicting ignition.
\end{itemize}

All preprocessing is fitted within the current training partition. The outer
test partition is not used to calculate medians, define category vocabularies,
estimate scaling factors, compute sample weights, select thresholds, or tune
hyperparameters.

The preprocessing sequence is:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Numeric values are parsed, converted to consistent SI units where
  appropriate, and checked for physically invalid values. Invalid or
  unparseable values are recorded as missing.

  \item Exact duplicate modelling rows are removed after standardisation, so
  equivalent values reported in different units are not retained as
  independent observations.

  \item For model families that require complete numeric inputs, missing numeric
  values are replaced using the median of the current training partition.
  XGBoost retains native numeric missing values.

  \item Missing categorical values are assigned an explicit \texttt{Unknown}
  level. Categorical variables are one-hot encoded using only categories
  observed in the training partition. A previously unseen category in a test
  partition does not create a new fitted column.

  \item Distance-based, margin-based, and neural-network models standardise
  numeric features using training-partition statistics. Tree-based models do
  not require standardisation.

  \item Paper weights, class weights, bootstrap-augmentation draws, and
  threshold-selection rules are calculated only after the outer training
  partition has been defined.
\end{enumerate}

This procedure prevents test-partition information from entering the fitted
representation and ensures that candidate differences reflect modelling choices
rather than leakage through preprocessing.

\subsection{Nested evaluation}

Nested evaluation separates model fitting, model selection, and final
performance measurement.

\begin{description}
  \item[Training data] fit model parameters, such as tree splits, regression
  coefficients, or neural-network weights.

  \item[Validation data] compare hyperparameter settings, such as tree depth,
  regularisation strength, neighbourhood size, or learning rate.

  \item[Test data] are held aside until the final evaluation. They are not used
  to fit the model, select hyperparameters, define preprocessing, or choose
  thresholds.
\end{description}

For each outer split, the procedure is:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Divide the available observations into an outer training partition and
  an outer test partition.

  \item Fit all preprocessing operations using only the outer training
  partition.

  \item Compare candidate hyperparameter configurations through inner
  cross-validation within that outer training partition.

  \item Select the best configuration using the declared inner-validation
  metric and tie-breaking policy.

  \item Refit the selected configuration using all observations in the outer
  training partition.

  \item Evaluate the refitted model once on the untouched outer test
  partition.
\end{enumerate}

All candidates within a task use the same frozen outer assignments. Their
scores can therefore be compared on identical test partitions. Repeated outer
splits quantify sensitivity to the particular division of the data, but their
scores are not treated as independent experimental replications.

\subsection{Interpolation versus extrapolation}

The pipelines answer two different questions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Interpolation} holds out rows while allowing other rows from
  the same paper to remain in training. It asks whether the model can predict
  new conditions within experimental campaigns it partly knows.

  \item \textbf{Extrapolation} holds out complete papers. It asks whether the
  model can transfer to a new publication, experimental rig, or campaign not
  represented during fitting.
\end{itemize}

The distinction is important because a source paper can contain clustered
observations from the same apparatus, material batch, measurement protocol, and
operating regime. A random row split may therefore reward recovery of
campaign-specific structure rather than transfer of general physical
relationships. All grouped protocols explicitly verify that no paper appears
in both the corresponding training and test partitions.

\subsection{Paper weighting, uncertainty, and refitting}

\subsubsection{Paper weighting}

Training minimises a loss: a numerical penalty that increases when a prediction
differs from the observed outcome. The empirical risk is the average training
loss. Without paper weighting, a paper with many rows contributes many more
terms to that average than a paper with only a few rows.

For model \(f\), row \(i\), observed outcome \(y_i\), prediction
\(f(\mathbf{x}_i)\), source paper \(p(i)\), and paper weight \(w_{p(i)}\), the
weighted empirical risk is

\[
  \widehat R_w(f)=
  \frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{T}}w_{p(i)}
  \ell\!\left(y_i,f(\mathbf{x}_i)\right)}
  {\sum_{i\in\mathcal{T}}w_{p(i)}}.
\]

The weight multiplies the loss for every row from a paper before the losses are
averaged. A larger total paper weight gives that paper greater influence during
training.

Both pipelines compare three weighting strategies
(\cref{fig:method-paper-weights}):

\begin{description}
  \item[Unweighted training: \(w_p=1\).]
  Every row has equal weight. Papers with more rows have greater total
  influence.

  \item[Square-root weighting: \(w_p=n_p^{-1/2}\).]
  Large papers retain more influence than small papers, but their advantage is
  reduced.

  \item[Inverse weighting: \(w_p=n_p^{-1}\).]
  Every paper has approximately equal total influence regardless of the number
  of rows it contributes.
\end{description}

Paper counts and weights are calculated from the current training partition
only. For model families that cannot accept sample weights directly, the
training rows are resampled deterministically according to the required
weighting policy so that the weighting condition is applied consistently.

\subsubsection{Uncertainty of reported performance}

Outer-fold means and standard deviations describe how performance changes across
different data partitions. The \(95\%\) percentile intervals use 2,000 bootstrap
replicates. The ignition and FSR pipelines use different procedures because
they store different evaluation outputs.

\begin{description}
  \item[Ignition classification.]
  Predictions for observations that appear in more than one repeated outer split
  are first averaged, leaving one measured label and one mean predicted
  probability per observation. Interpolation intervals resample these unique
  observations with replacement; extrapolation and leave-one-paper-out intervals
  resample complete canonical-paper clusters. ROC--AUC, PR--AUC, and Brier score
  are recalculated for each of 2,000 replicates, and the \(95\%\) interval spans
  the 2.5th to the 97.5th percentile. Threshold-dependent ignition metrics are
  reported without bootstrap intervals in the current implementation.

  \item[FSR regression.]
  The FSR pipeline stores measured and predicted FSR values for every outer-test
  observation. When an observation appears in more than one repeated outer
  split, its predictions are averaged first, leaving one measured--predicted
  pair per observation.

  For interpolation, bootstrap replicates resample these unique observations
  with replacement and recalculate RMSE, MAE, and \(R^2\).

  For extrapolation, bootstrap replicates resample complete canonical-paper
  clusters with replacement. When a paper is drawn, all of its observations
  enter the replicate; when it is drawn more than once, all its observations
  are repeated accordingly. RMSE, MAE, and \(R^2\) are then recalculated from
  the resulting paper-cluster sample.
\end{description}

These intervals quantify resampling uncertainty within the assembled database.
They do not include uncertainty from literature selection, transcription,
measurement error, or unobserved experimental conditions.

\subsubsection{Final refitting and champion selection}

Candidate selection is completed before any full-data model is fitted. A
candidate can be called a champion only if it appears in the persisted selection
evidence and passes the required integrity checks.

After selection, the chosen configuration is refitted using all labelled rows
for its task. This full-data model is the inference artifact used for future
predictions. Its training performance is not evidence of generalisation; only
the nested outer-fold results support comparative performance claims.

The merged FSR comparison contains 25 of the 34 configured candidates. The
formal ignition selection record does not include the available MLP shards.
Consequently, an unevaluated or incompletely persisted candidate cannot become
a reported champion through configuration alone.

\subsection{Reproducibility and audit trail}
\label{sec:method-reproducibility}

Reproducibility is based on persisted artifacts rather than narrative
description alone. Each cleaned task-specific dataset is fingerprinted before
split generation. Outer and inner split assignments are created from declared
seeds, stored with canonical paper identifiers, and reused by every candidate.

For every candidate and outer split, the pipeline stores:

\begin{itemize}
  \item candidate identity, feature manifest, paper-weighting policy, and
  augmentation condition;
  \item selected hyperparameters and inner-validation history;
  \item fold-level metrics and row-level outer-test predictions;
  \item paper-level metrics where applicable;
  \item integrity checks, including expected-fold completion, finite metrics,
  valid classifier probabilities, and zero paper overlap for grouped splits.
\end{itemize}

Candidate selection reads these artifacts without fitting a new model.
Candidates with incomplete folds, invalid predictions, or failed integrity
checks are excluded from the relevant comparison. The selected configuration is
refitted only after this evaluation record is complete.

Evaluation artifacts and refitted artifacts have different roles. Outer-fold
predictions provide the comparison evidence because they are generated on
untouched test partitions. The refitted model is trained on all labelled rows
and is intended for inference; its training fit is not used as evidence of
generalisation.

\subsection{Explainability and physical interpretation}
\label{sec:method-explainability}

Explainability is performed after model selection. It does not influence
candidate ranking, hyperparameter optimisation, threshold selection, or model
choice. Its purpose is to inspect how the fitted model uses available features.

Two complementary methods are used:

\begin{description}

  \item[Permutation importance.]
  Permutation importance measures the loss of held-out performance after one
  original feature is shuffled while all other features remain unchanged. For
  ignition, the loss is measured using held-out ROC--AUC. For FSR, it is
  measured using the increase in held-out RMSE.

  \item[SHAP values.]
  SHAP decomposes a fitted prediction into a baseline value and additive feature
  contributions:
  \[
  f(\mathbf{x}_i)
  =
  \phi_0+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\phi_{ij},
  \]
  where \(f(\mathbf{x}_i)\) is the prediction for observation \(i\),
  \(\phi_0\) is the model baseline, and \(\phi_{ij}\) is the contribution of
  feature \(j\) to that prediction.
\end{description}

The global mean absolute SHAP importance of feature \(j\) is

\[
I^{\mathrm{SHAP}}_j
=
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\phi_{ij}|.
\]

A large value of \(I^{\mathrm{SHAP}}_j\) indicates that a feature changes model
predictions strongly on average. It does not indicate whether the feature
increases or decreases a prediction.

SHAP beeswarm plots retain this directional information. Each point represents
one observation. Its horizontal position is the SHAP value: positive values
push a prediction upward and negative values push it downward. For ignition,
positive values increase the predicted probability of ignition; negative values
push the prediction toward no ignition. For FSR, positive values increase the
predicted flame-spread rate and negative values decrease it. Point colour shows
the original feature value: red denotes high values and blue denotes low values.

These methods explain the fitted predictive function; they do not establish
causal combustion mechanisms.

\section{Ignition-classification pipeline}
\label{sec:classification-method}

\subsection{Task, probabilities, and metrics}

Ignition is a binary target. Each observation is labelled ignition
(\(y=1\)) or no ignition (\(y=0\)). The classifier returns an estimated
probability of ignition, \(\widehat p\in[0,1]\), rather than a direct physical
yes/no decision.

A decision threshold \(\tau\) converts this probability into a binary
prediction:

\[
\widehat y(\tau)=
\begin{cases}
1, & \widehat p\geq\tau,\\
0, & \widehat p<\tau.
\end{cases}
\]

Lowering the threshold identifies more potential ignitions but usually
increases false alarms. Raising it reduces false alarms but increases the risk
of missing true ignitions. Each threshold therefore creates a different
combination of true positives (TP), false positives (FP), true negatives (TN),
and false negatives (FN).

The principal threshold-dependent quantities are:

\begin{description}
  \item[Sensitivity or recall.]
  The fraction of true ignitions correctly identified:
  \[
  \mathrm{sensitivity}
  =\mathrm{recall}
  =\frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN}}.
  \]

  \item[Specificity.]
  The fraction of true non-ignitions correctly identified:
  \[
  \mathrm{specificity}
  =\frac{\mathrm{TN}}{\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FP}}.
  \]

  \item[False-positive rate.]
  The fraction of true non-ignitions incorrectly classified as ignition:
  \[
  \mathrm{FPR}
  =1-\mathrm{specificity}
  =\frac{\mathrm{FP}}{\mathrm{FP}+\mathrm{TN}}.
  \]

  \item[Precision.]
  The fraction of predicted ignitions that actually ignite:
  \[
  \mathrm{precision}
  =\frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP}}.
  \]
\end{description}

The ignition dataset is majority-positive, so accuracy is not a suitable
selection metric. A classifier that predicts ignition for every observation
would achieve a high accuracy while providing no useful discrimination between
ignition and no-ignition conditions.

Candidate comparison therefore begins with threshold-independent metrics:

\begin{description}
  \item[ROC--AUC.]
  The receiver-operating-characteristic curve plots sensitivity against the
  false-positive rate across all possible thresholds. ROC--AUC is the
  probability that a randomly selected ignition observation receives a higher
  predicted probability than a randomly selected no-ignition observation. A
  no-skill ranking model has ROC--AUC equal to \(0.5\).

  \item[PR--AUC.]
  The precision--recall curve plots precision against recall across all
  possible thresholds. Its no-skill baseline is the positive-class prevalence,
  not \(0.5\). PR--AUC therefore measures whether the model provides useful
  positive-class ranking beyond an always-ignite policy.

  \item[Brier score.]
  The Brier score measures squared probability error:
  \[
  \mathrm{BS}
  =\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}
  \left(\widehat p_i-y_i\right)^2.
  \]
  Lower values indicate predicted probabilities that are closer to the
  observed labels.
\end{description}

Four threshold-selection objectives are also evaluated:

\begin{description}
  \item[F1 score.]
  The harmonic mean of precision and recall:
  \[
  \mathrm{F1}
  =\frac{2\,\mathrm{precision}\cdot\mathrm{recall}}
  {\mathrm{precision}+\mathrm{recall}}.
  \]

  \item[Balanced accuracy.]
  The mean of sensitivity and specificity:
  \[
  \mathrm{balanced\ accuracy}
  =\frac{\mathrm{sensitivity}+\mathrm{specificity}}{2}.
  \]

  \item[Youden's \(J\).]
  The difference between sensitivity and false-positive rate:
  \[
  J=\mathrm{sensitivity}+\mathrm{specificity}-1.
  \]

  \item[Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC).]
  A summary based on all four confusion-matrix counts:
  \[
  \mathrm{MCC}
  =\frac{\mathrm{TP}\,\mathrm{TN}-\mathrm{FP}\,\mathrm{FN}}
  {\sqrt{(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP})(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN})
  (\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FP})(\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FN})}}.
  \]
\end{description}

ROC--AUC, PR--AUC, and Brier score are used for candidate comparison because
they do not require a single operating threshold. Within each outer training
partition, thresholds that maximise MCC, F1, balanced accuracy, and Youden's
\(J\) are selected from inner out-of-fold predictions. Those thresholds are
then frozen and applied once to the untouched outer test partition. Outer-test
labels are never used to choose a threshold.

Thresholded metrics describe possible operating points rather than a single
universally optimal fire-safety decision. The appropriate threshold depends on
the relative consequences of a missed ignition and a false alarm.

\subsection{Candidate design}

The ignition portfolio contains 26 candidates across five model families
(\cref{fig:method-ignition-xgb,fig:method-ignition-portfolio}). The portfolio
tests how each family responds to paper-size imbalance, class imbalance, and
specific structural choices. It is not an unrestricted search over unrelated
architectures.

Every family includes an unweighted baseline. XGBoost receives the most detailed
ablation because it is the primary tabular learner. The remaining families
provide structured benchmarks under the same nested evaluation procedure.

Paper weighting and class weighting address different sources of imbalance.
Paper weighting reduces the influence of densely sampled campaigns. Class
weighting changes the relative penalty assigned to ignition and no-ignition
errors. Focal loss \cite{lin2017focal} is a separate class-imbalance strategy:
it down-weights easy, high-confidence examples and places greater emphasis on
harder examples. Focal candidates use \(\gamma=2\) and
\texttt{class\_weight:false} to avoid combining two class-imbalance
corrections. Paper weighting remains active because it addresses source-size
imbalance rather than class frequency.

\subsection{Pipeline procedure}

Every ignition candidate follows the same sequence:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Load the cleaned ignition dataset and fixed feature manifest. Paper
  identity is retained for grouping, weighting, and integrity checks, but is
  never supplied as a predictor.

  \item Freeze the outer evaluation assignments before comparing candidates.
  The stored protocols include repeated stratified interpolation
  cross-validation, a stratified 80/20 consistency holdout, repeated
  paper-grouped extrapolation, and leave-one-paper-out analysis for the
  extrapolation champion.

  \item For one candidate and one outer split, fit preprocessing and evaluate
  hyperparameter configurations using only the outer training partition.

  \item Generate inner out-of-fold probabilities and select four operating
  thresholds: maximum MCC, maximum F1, maximum balanced accuracy, and maximum
  Youden's \(J\).

  \item Refit the selected configuration using the complete outer training
  partition, then predict the untouched outer test partition.

  \item Calculate threshold-independent and threshold-dependent metrics using
  the frozen thresholds and outer-test predictions.

  \item Persist outer-fold predictions, fold metrics, selected
  hyperparameters, threshold values, and integrity checks.

  \item Select separate interpolation and extrapolation champions from the
  persisted evidence, then refit those selected configurations on all labelled
  ignition rows for inference.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Interpolation protocol}

Ignition interpolation uses five-fold stratified cross-validation repeated for
seeds 42--44, giving 15 outer folds. Stratification keeps the ignition and
no-ignition proportions similar across folds. Rows are assigned independently,
so observations from one paper may appear in both training and test partitions.

Within each outer training partition, stratified inner folds select
hyperparameters and threshold policies. A separate stratified 80/20 holdout is
also generated for each seed as a consistency check, but it is not the primary
selection protocol.

Interpolation candidates must produce valid probabilities, complete every
expected fold, and pass integrity checks. They must also have a ROC--AUC
fold-level standard deviation no greater than 0.15. Candidates within 0.01 of
the best mean ROC--AUC are resolved by higher PR--AUC, lower uncertainty, and
the declared simplicity order: decision tree, \(k\)-NN, SVM, XGBoost, then MLP.
The selected interpolation configuration is refitted on all labelled ignition
rows for within-domain inference.

\subsection{Extrapolation and leave-one-paper-out}

Ignition extrapolation uses \texttt{StratifiedGroupKFold}. All rows from one
paper remain together, while each fold retains both ignition classes where
feasible. Five paper-grouped folds repeated across three seeds produce 15 outer
test partitions.

The inner search is also paper-disjoint. A paper used for inner validation
cannot appear in the corresponding inner training fold. This prevents
hyperparameter selection and threshold selection from benefiting from rows from
the paper later used for outer testing.

The primary extrapolation metric remains ROC--AUC. Eligibility additionally
requires a successful leave-one-paper-out analysis. Each of the 87 canonical
papers is held out once. A paper containing one ignition class cannot define its
own ROC--AUC, so LOPO is interpreted through pooled predictions, calibration,
and the subset of papers for which class-discriminating metrics are defined.

The extrapolation champion and its threshold policies are refitted on the full
labelled ignition cohort only after the paper-disjoint comparison is complete.

\section{Flame-spread-rate regression pipeline}
\label{sec:regression-method}

\subsection{Task and metrics}

The FSR pipeline predicts continuous flame-spread rate in
\si{\metre\per\second}. Unlike ignition classification, regression does not
require a probability threshold: each prediction is compared directly with the
measured FSR value.

For observation \(i\), let \(y_i\) be the measured FSR and
\(\widehat y_i\) the predicted FSR. The residual is

\[
e_i=\widehat y_i-y_i.
\]

A positive residual indicates overprediction; a negative residual indicates
underprediction. The principal regression metrics are:

\begin{description}
  \item[Root-mean-square error (RMSE).]
  \[
  \mathrm{RMSE}
  =
  \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}
  \left(y_i-\widehat y_i\right)^2}.
  \]
  RMSE is expressed in \si{\metre\per\second} and gives greater weight to
  large prediction errors. It is the primary selection metric because it
  directly represents the physical scale of the error.

  \item[Mean absolute error (MAE).]
  \[
  \mathrm{MAE}
  =
  \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}
  \left|y_i-\widehat y_i\right|.
  \]
  MAE is also expressed in \si{\metre\per\second}. It describes typical
  absolute error and is less sensitive than RMSE to a small number of large
  misses.

  \item[Coefficient of determination (\(R^2\)).]
  \[
  R^2
  =
  1-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-\widehat y_i)^2}
  {\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-\bar y)^2}.
  \]
  \(R^2\) compares the model with a predictor that always returns the mean
  FSR. A value of \(R^2=1\) indicates a perfect fit, whereas \(R^2=0\)
  performs no better than the mean predictor. Because \(R^2\) depends on the
  target variation within the evaluated data, it can differ across held-out
  paper groups even when absolute errors are similar.

  \item[Mean bias error (MBE).]
  \[
  \mathrm{MBE}
  =
  \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}
  \left(\widehat y_i-y_i\right).
  \]
  MBE identifies systematic overprediction or underprediction.

  \item[Mean absolute percentage error (MAPE).]
  MAPE is retained as a secondary diagnostic only. It becomes unstable when
  measured FSR approaches zero and is therefore not used for candidate
  selection.
\end{description}

All predictions are converted back to the original FSR scale before scoring.
This includes candidates trained with the signed-log target transformation.
Consequently, every reported RMSE, MAE, and MBE value remains interpretable in
physical FSR units.

RMSE and \(R^2\) answer complementary questions. RMSE is the preferred metric
for an engineering error budget because it is reported in
\si{\metre\per\second}. \(R^2\) indicates whether the model captures variation
beyond a mean predictor, but it depends on the FSR spread of the held-out data.
The two metrics are therefore interpreted together rather than treated as
interchangeable.

Interpolation and paper-grouped extrapolation also answer different questions.
Interpolation evaluates new rows from papers that may otherwise be represented
in training. Paper-grouped extrapolation evaluates transfer to entirely unseen
publications or experimental campaigns. These scores must not be averaged or
substituted for one another; paper-grouped performance is the primary internal
evidence for transfer beyond represented source papers.

Rivera, Jose, and colleagues reported \(R^2>0.9\) values for a narrower
electrical-wire dataset under random ten-fold cross-validation
\cite{riveraWireML}. The present pipeline reports a comparable interpolation
result, but its scientific generalisation claim is based on the paper-grouped
evaluation.

\subsection{Candidate design}

The FSR portfolio contains 34 candidates across five model families
(\cref{fig:method-fsr-xgb,fig:method-fsr-portfolio}). Each candidate tests a
specific modelling hypothesis: whether paper weighting improves transfer,
whether robust losses reduce sensitivity to outliers, whether target
transformation helps with a skewed FSR distribution, whether local similarity
is informative, or whether the engineered physics feature space is sufficiently
linear.

Linear, elastic-net, SGD, Bayesian-ridge, and kernel-ridge models are not
included because they do not add a new modelling question beyond the previous
benchmark study. Random forest is added as a bagging-based comparison with
XGBoost so that any preference for tree ensembles is not attributed to
boosting alone.

XGBoost contains the most detailed ablation, with 10 candidates. Squared-error,
absolute-error, and pseudo-Huber objectives are compared under square-root and
inverse paper weighting. Absolute error reduces the influence of large residuals,
whereas pseudo-Huber behaves quadratically near zero and approximately linearly
for large residuals. Two candidates use the signed-log target transformation,

\[
\mathrm{sign}(y)\log(1+|y|),
\]

which is inverted before scoring. These candidates test whether compressing the
right-skewed FSR distribution improves optimisation. One further candidate uses
paper-level bagging, which averages ten XGBoost models trained on paper-level
bootstrap samples.

\subsection{Pipeline procedure}

Every FSR candidate is evaluated under two training-data conditions:

\begin{description}
  \item[\texttt{rd}.]
  The selected configuration is trained using only the real observations from
  the outer training partition.

  \item[\texttt{rd\_bt}.]
  The same real training observations are retained and 1,000 additional rows
  are sampled with replacement from that outer training partition. Each copy
  keeps its original predictors, FSR target, and source-paper identity. Test
  observations are never augmented.
\end{description}

The pipeline follows the same sequence for every candidate:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Load the cleaned FSR dataset and retain only rows with a numeric FSR
  target in \si{\metre\per\second}.

  \item Freeze outer evaluation assignments before comparing candidates.
  Interpolation uses repeated approximately 80/20 row-level holdouts.
  Extrapolation uses repeated approximately 80/20 paper-grouped holdouts.

  \item For interpolation, FSR values are binned into six target quantiles
  where feasible during split construction. This helps represent the
  right-skewed FSR distribution in both training and test partitions.

  \item For extrapolation, complete canonical papers are assigned to either
  training or testing. Candidate paper-disjoint partitions are generated for
  each repeat, and the most target-balanced partition is retained.

  \item Select hyperparameters using only the outer training partition.
  Interpolation uses shuffled inner folds; extrapolation uses paper-disjoint
  inner folds.

  \item Fit each selected configuration under the raw-data and bootstrap-
  augmented conditions, then predict the untouched outer test partition.

  \item Calculate RMSE, MAE, \(R^2\), MBE, and other diagnostics after
  converting predictions back to the original FSR scale.

  \item Persist outer-test predictions, fold metrics, selected
  hyperparameters, inner-search histories, and integrity records for every
  eligible candidate, protocol, and training-data condition.

  \item Merge candidate shards after evaluation. Select separate interpolation
  and extrapolation champions from the persisted evidence, then refit those
  selected configurations on all labelled FSR rows for inference.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Interpolation protocol}

FSR interpolation uses ten repeated, approximately 80/20 row-level holdouts
with seeds 42--51. FSR values are binned into six quantiles where feasible so
that each split represents the strongly right-skewed target distribution.
Observations from the same paper may appear in both the training and test
partitions.

Within each outer training partition, shuffled \(K\)-fold validation compares
hyperparameter configurations. For a typical repeat, the selected
configuration is fitted on approximately 2,024 training rows and tested on
approximately 507 untouched rows.

The selection policy rejects incomplete or non-finite evaluations, RMSE
coefficient of variation above 0.45, and mean \(R^2<-0.25\). Candidates within
2\% of the lowest mean RMSE form a tie band. Ties are resolved by higher
\(R^2\), lower uncertainty, preference for raw data over bootstrap augmentation,
the declared model-simplicity order---random forest, \(k\)-NN, SVR, XGBoost,
then MLP---and fewer features where feature sets differ.

\subsection{Extrapolation protocol}

FSR extrapolation also uses ten repeated approximately 80/20 holdouts, but the
assignment unit is the canonical paper rather than the individual row. For each
repeat, 50 candidate \texttt{GroupShuffleSplit} partitions are generated and
the most target-balanced paper-disjoint split is retained. Each test partition
contains 14 papers and each training partition contains 52 papers, although row
counts vary because papers contribute different numbers of observations.

Balance screening reduces avoidable changes in FSR range between training and
test data without relaxing paper separation. The screening uses only
target-distribution summaries during split construction and is completed before
any candidate is fitted. The resulting score is therefore a transfer estimate
for this balance-screened split policy, not for every possible future set of
papers.

Inner tuning uses \texttt{GroupKFold} by paper. Extrapolation tie-breaking also
considers the interpolation--extrapolation gap so that a candidate is not
favoured solely because it performs well within represented papers. Bootstrap
uncertainty intervals resample complete papers rather than treating their rows
as independent observations.

The selected
\((\mathrm{candidate},\,\mathrm{augmentation})\) pair is refitted on all
2,531 labelled FSR rows only after the paper-grouped comparison is complete.
The resulting full-data model is stored separately from the outer-fold evidence
used to support performance claims.
