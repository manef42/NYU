\chapter{Results}
\label{chap:results}

After the preprocessing described in \cref{chap:methodology}, the ignition
cohort contains 4,487 labeled rows from 87 papers and the FSR cohort contains
2,531 numeric observations from 66 papers. The figures below report those
modeling tasks. Database sampling geometry that is not itself a predictive
score---in particular oxygen--pressure coverage---is interpreted in
\cref{sec:discussion_applications} as a map of the experimental search space
that remains to be filled.

\section{Ignition classification}
\label{sec:results-ignition}

\subsection{ROC, precision--recall, and the fire-safe operating question}
\label{sec:ignition-auc}

A binary ignition model does not output a physical yes/no switch. It outputs a
score \(\widehat p\in[0,1]\), interpreted as an estimated probability of
ignition. Converting that score into a decision requires a threshold
\(\tau\):
\begin{equation}
  \widehat y(\tau)=
  \begin{cases}
    1,& \widehat p\geq\tau,\\
    0,& \widehat p<\tau.
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Each \(\tau\) produces a confusion-matrix count of true positives (TP), false
positives (FP), true negatives (TN), and false negatives (FN). The elementary
rates used throughout this section are
\begin{align}
  \mathrm{TPR}
  =\mathrm{sensitivity}
  =\mathrm{recall}
  &=\frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN}},\\
  \mathrm{FPR}
  &=1-\mathrm{specificity}
  =\frac{\mathrm{FP}}{\mathrm{FP}+\mathrm{TN}},\\
  \mathrm{precision}
  &=\frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP}}.
\end{align}
Recall is the fraction of true ignitions that are caught. Precision is the
fraction of predicted ignitions that actually ignite. Specificity is the
fraction of true non-ignitions that are left unlabeled. No single rate is a
complete account of a fire-safety call: missing an ignition (false negative)
is the safety-critical error, while flooding a test queue with false alarms
(false positives) makes the screener unusable.

Two threshold-independent curves summarize the whole family of operating
points. The receiver-operating-characteristic (ROC) curve plots TPR against
FPR as \(\tau\) varies. A no-skill classifier that ranks at random traces the
diagonal, with area \(1/2\). The area under the ROC curve (ROC--AUC) equals
the probability that a randomly chosen ignition receives a higher score than a
randomly chosen non-ignition. It is a ranking metric: it does not select
\(\tau\), and it weights the two classes symmetrically through TPR and FPR.

The precision--recall (PR) curve plots precision against recall. Its no-skill
baseline is not \(1/2\); it is the positive prevalence
\(\pi=P(y=1)\). In this database \(\pi\approx 0.74\)--\(0.75\), so an
always-ignite rule already attains precision equal to that prevalence and
recall equal to one. The area under the PR curve (PR--AUC, equivalently
average precision in \cref{fig:ignition-auc}) therefore measures how much
ranking quality exists \emph{above} that naive majority policy.

A convenient single-threshold summary of the PR trade-off is the F1 score, the
harmonic mean of precision and recall,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{F1}
  =\frac{2\,\mathrm{precision}\cdot\mathrm{recall}}
        {\mathrm{precision}+\mathrm{recall}}.
\end{equation}
Because a harmonic mean is dominated by the smaller of its two arguments, F1
is large only when both precision and recall are large. It does not, however,
use true negatives, so a majority-positive task can reward a low \(\tau\) that
labels almost everything as ignition. Complementary summaries that do use the
full confusion matrix are balanced accuracy
\((\mathrm{sensitivity}+\mathrm{specificity})/2\), Youden's
\(J=\mathrm{sensitivity}+\mathrm{specificity}-1\), and the Matthews
correlation
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{MCC}
  =\frac{\mathrm{TP}\,\mathrm{TN}-\mathrm{FP}\,\mathrm{FN}}
        {\sqrt{(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP})(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN})
        (\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FP})(\mathrm{TN}+\mathrm{FN})}}.
\end{equation}
Thresholds that maximize these criteria are selected inside training folds and
are interpreted as operational limitations in
\cref{sec:discussion_limitations}; they are not used to claim a unique
``best'' fire-safety setting.

\Cref{fig:ignition-auc} shows both curves for the selected XGBoost classifier
on a held-out partition. The ROC curve rises steeply toward the upper-left
corner and attains AUC \(0.897\), well above the chance diagonal. That result
says the model ranks ignitions above non-ignitions for most pairs. It does
\emph{not} by itself say that a fire-safe call is available: a high ROC--AUC
can coexist with many false positives when negatives are the minority class,
because FPR is computed only among true non-ignitions.

The PR curve is the more informative fire-safety diagnostic for exactly that
reason. Precision remains near one up to recall of about \(0.7\), then
declines as the threshold is lowered to recover the remaining ignitions.
Average precision is \(0.963\), far above the prevalence baseline of \(0.74\)
drawn as the dashed line. In operational language: there exists a high-
precision region in which flagged cases are very likely to be true ignitions,
and the model can recover a large majority of ignitions before precision
collapses. Pushing recall toward one---the instinctive ``never miss a
fire'' policy---still costs precision, which is why PR--AUC, rather than
ROC--AUC or accuracy, is the curve that should govern a screening claim on
this imbalanced label. ROC--AUC remains useful as a threshold-free ranking
score for model comparison; PR--AUC is the quantity that asks whether
positive-class decisions remain reliable.

\subsection{Interpolation and extrapolation by model family}

The selected interpolation classifier is the paper-weighted XGBoost recipe
\texttt{xgb\_paper\_sqrt}. Across 15 repeated stratified outer folds it
reaches ROC--AUC \(0.9076\pm0.0102\), PR--AUC \(0.9687\pm0.0039\), and mean
Brier score \(0.1063\). The selected extrapolation classifier is the
unweighted \texttt{xgb\_baseline}. Its paper-grouped ROC--AUC is
\(0.7183\pm0.0444\), PR--AUC \(0.8767\pm0.0298\), and Brier score \(0.1791\).
The paper-cluster bootstrap estimate of grouped ROC--AUC is \(0.7101\) with
95\% interval \(0.6408\)--\(0.7848\); leave-one-paper-out robustness is
similar (\(0.7020\), \(0.6376\)--\(0.7751\)).

\Cref{fig:ignition-family-auc} presents the same two questions for the best
persisted candidate in each family, so interpolation and extrapolation are
read together rather than as separate leaderboards. Panel (a) uses ROC--AUC;
panel (b) uses PR--AUC. In every family the interpolation marker lies to the
right of the extrapolation marker, with much smaller error bars. XGBoost is
the strongest family on ROC--AUC for both protocols (interpolation
\(0.901\pm0.002\), extrapolation \(0.718\pm0.044\) on the plotted
representatives). SVM and MLP occupy a middle band; decision trees and
\(k\)-nearest neighbors are weaker, and the decision-tree extrapolation bar is
the most unstable (\(\mathrm{SD}=0.100\)).

PR--AUC preserves the same family ranking for interpolation, with XGBoost at
\(0.966\pm0.001\). Under extrapolation the picture is slightly different: SVM
reaches \(0.879\pm0.022\) and XGBoost \(0.877\pm0.030\), so the two families
are indistinguishable on the positive-class ranking metric once papers are
held out. That is consistent with the fire-safety reading of
\cref{fig:ignition-auc}: ROC--AUC and PR--AUC can disagree about a close call
because they answer different questions. All families remain well above the
prevalence baseline of \(\approx 0.75\), but the drop from interpolation
(\(\mathrm{PR}\)--\(\mathrm{AUC}\approx 0.93\)--\(0.97\)) to extrapolation
(\(\approx 0.82\)--\(0.88\)) is the quantity that limits transfer to a new
campaign. Tree-depth flexibility (XGBoost versus a single decision tree) and
margin-based models (SVM) retain more of that positive-class ranking than
memory-based KNN or an unpruned tree.

Probability ranking is not calibration. \Cref{fig:ignition-calibration}
compares mean predicted probability with the observed ignition fraction. The
interpolation champion tracks the identity line closely, especially above
probability \(0.5\). The extrapolation champion is miscalibrated: its scores
occupy a narrower, higher band, and high predicted probabilities overstate
the observed ignition rate. A numerical probability from the grouped model
therefore cannot be read as a frequentist ignition risk without a
source-conditional calibration step.

\subsection{Ignition explainability}

The held-paper XGBoost SHAP plot in \cref{fig:ignition-explainability} shows
both contribution direction and feature value. Oxygen has the largest global
SHAP magnitude, followed by absolute flow speed, Prandtl number, gas molar
mass, fuel pyrolysis temperature, and ignition time. Higher oxygen values
generally move predictions toward ignition in this fitted model. Low absolute
flow speed produces large negative SHAP values, consistent with a quenching
branch in which weak transport cannot sustain a flame. Long recorded ignition
times also push the score downward, while several transport quantities show
nonlinear or mixed patterns.

Permutation importance gives a different ranking: fuel pyrolysis temperature,
ignition time, fuel heat capacity, and thermal diffusion time produce the
largest positive held-out ROC--AUC losses when shuffled. Oxygen has high SHAP
magnitude but is not the leading permutation feature in this held-out sample.
The disagreement is evidence of correlated and substitutable predictors, not a
contradiction and not a causal ranking. Oxygen, flow, and thermal time scales
nonetheless recur across both views, which is the level of interpretation the
observational table can support.

\section{Flame-spread-rate regression}
\label{sec:results-fsr}

\subsection{Interpolation and extrapolation together}

Regression has no classification threshold: the model predicts a continuous
FSR in \si{\metre\per\second}, and quality is measured in those units. Two
summaries are used throughout. RMSE is the root-mean-square error
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{RMSE}
  =\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-\widehat y_i)^2},
\end{equation}
which penalizes large misses and remains dimensionally an FSR. The coefficient
of determination
\begin{equation}
  R^2
  =1-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-\widehat y_i)^2}
          {\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-\bar y)^2}
\end{equation}
compares residual variance with a mean predictor; \(R^2=1\) is a perfect fit
and \(R^2=0\) is no better than predicting \(\bar y\). The two metrics are
not interchangeable. RMSE is the engineering error in physical units; \(R^2\)
is a scale-free statement about explained variance and is sensitive to the
target spread of the held-out papers. Ranking models by RMSE can therefore
disagree with ranking them by \(R^2\) when folds differ in variance, which is
why both axes appear below.

The unaugmented random-forest baseline is selected for both protocols, with
separately tuned configurations. Across ten repeated row holdouts it reaches
\(R^2=0.8790\pm0.0178\),
RMSE \(0.006263\pm0.000472~\si{\metre\per\second}\), and
MAE \(0.002103\pm0.000173~\si{\metre\per\second}\). Across ten paper-grouped
holdouts the same family reaches
\(R^2=0.4576\pm0.2215\),
RMSE \(0.012205\pm0.004115~\si{\metre\per\second}\), and
MAE \(0.004819\pm0.001144~\si{\metre\per\second}\). Grouped RMSE is 1.95 times
the interpolation value; the paper-cluster 95\% interval for grouped RMSE is
\(0.006525\)--\(0.017839~\si{\metre\per\second}\). Bootstrap-augmented XGBoost
has a numerically slightly lower interpolation RMSE
(\(0.006236~\si{\metre\per\second}\)) but falls within the 2\% tie band and
has greater fold variability, so the declared tie policy favors the
unaugmented random forest.

\Cref{fig:fsr-model-comparison} shows every persisted candidate under both
protocols and both training conditions (real data versus bootstrap-augmented
training). Interpolation bars (blue) are consistently lower than grouped bars
(orange). Tree ensembles occupy the left-hand, low-RMSE group; KNN occupies a
middle band; SVR and MLP occupy the right-hand, high-error group, with
grouped error bars that can exceed \(0.03~\si{\metre\per\second}\).
Augmentation does not close the protocol gap: the dark and light bars of a
given protocol remain close relative to the blue--orange separation. The
figure therefore answers interpolation and extrapolation in one comparison:
family identity and evaluation protocol dominate recipe-level tweaks.

\Cref{fig:fsr-tradeoff} makes the two ranking metrics explicit. Each point is
a candidate; the horizontal axis is mean \(R^2\) (higher is better) and the
vertical axis is mean RMSE (lower is better), so the desirable corner is the
bottom right. A dashed line separates grouped extrapolation (left, purple)
from random interpolation (right, blue). Three facts follow immediately.

First, the two metrics largely agree inside each protocol: points fall along
a descending cloud, so improving \(R^2\) generally reduces RMSE. There are
nevertheless two legitimate ways to rank models. RMSE is the quantity to
quote for a physical error budget; \(R^2\) is the quantity to quote when
asking whether the model beat a mean predictor on that fold's target spread.
A candidate can look competitive on one axis and less so on the other if its
errors concentrate on high-variance papers.

Second, model families are visually separated in both panels. Random forest
and XGBoost occupy the lower-right cluster of each protocol; KNN occupies an
intermediate cluster; SVR and MLP occupy the upper-left cluster. Family
identity is therefore a stronger organizing principle than individual
hyperparameter variants. The highlighted random-forest baseline
(\(R^2=0.879\), RMSE \(=0.00626~\si{\metre\per\second}\) in interpolation;
\(R^2=0.458\), RMSE \(=0.01221~\si{\metre\per\second}\) in extrapolation) sits
at the front of the tree cluster in both tasks.

Third, the protocol shift is a translation of the entire family structure,
not a reordering. The same relative ranking survives paper hold-out, but the
absolute location moves from \(R^2\approx 0.4\)--\(0.9\) to
\(R^2\approx 0.05\)--\(0.46\) and from RMSE \(\approx 0.006\)--\(0.015\) to
RMSE \(\approx 0.012\)--\(0.016~\si{\metre\per\second}\). Interpolation
competence does not imply transfer; family ranking, however, is stable enough
that a screening tool can prefer tree ensembles without pretending that
within-paper \(R^2=0.88\) is available for a new campaign.

\subsection{Held-out parity and error structure}

The two panels of \cref{fig:fsr-parity-error} diagnose where the grouped
random forest succeeds and where it fails. Panel (a) is the extrapolation
parity plot: predicted versus experimental FSR, with the one-to-one line as
the reference. The pooled snapshot on that figure is \(R^2=0.425\) and
RMSE \(=0.0129~\si{\metre\per\second}\), consistent with the repeated-holdout
mean \(R^2=0.458\pm0.222\). Dense low-FSR observations lie near the diagonal.
Above about \(0.05~\si{\metre\per\second}\) the cloud peels away below the
line: experimental values continue to \(0.21~\si{\metre\per\second}\) while
predictions rarely exceed \(0.08~\si{\metre\per\second}\). The model
regresses toward the densely sampled low-spread mean.

Panel (b) plots the same predictions as error (predicted minus experimental)
against true FSR. At near-zero spread the errors are small and slightly
positive: the model overpredicts the slowest flames. As experimental FSR
increases, the error trend is a descending line that reaches almost
\(-0.20~\si{\metre\per\second}\) at the fastest observations, meaning a
high-spread event is predicted near zero. Error variance also widens with
true FSR (heteroscedasticity). For a fire-safety regressor this pattern is
the opposite of a conservative bias: the dangerous tail is underpredicted.
The hardest held-out paper reaches RMSE
\(0.1331~\si{\metre\per\second}\), whereas many papers remain below
\(0.003~\si{\metre\per\second}\). A single pooled \(R^2\) therefore conceals
campaign-level failure modes that the error plot makes visible.

\subsection{FSR explainability}

The FSR random forest relies most strongly on thermal diffusion time, oxygen,
gas thermal conductivity, and flow velocity in its SHAP summary
(\cref{fig:fsr-explainability}). The beeswarm adds direction: high oxygen
generally pushes FSR upward, while large thermal diffusion times---a thick or
thermally inertial solid---push FSR downward. Held-out permutation importance
independently identifies oxygen, flow velocity, gas conductivity, and thermal
diffusion time as the largest positive RMSE degradations.

Several correlated material variables have negative permutation importance in
the realized held-out sample. That means shuffling happened to reduce error;
it does not imply a physically protective effect. The repeated appearance of
oxygen, flow, and thermal transport across two explanation methods supports
their use as hypotheses and coverage priorities, not as causal coefficients.

\section{Synthesis}

The figures establish one common result across two targets and different model
families: within-paper interpolation is strong, while transfer to an unseen
paper is moderate and substantially more uncertain. Ignition ROC--AUC falls
from approximately \(0.91\) to \(0.72\), and PR--AUC from \(0.97\) to
\(0.88\); FSR RMSE approximately doubles and \(R^2\) falls from \(0.879\) to
\(0.458\). Family identity is stable: tree ensembles lead both tasks on both
protocols. These protocol effects are larger than the differences among the
leading tree recipes.

PR--AUC, not ROC--AUC, is the ignition summary that matches a fire-safe
screening call, because the label is majority-positive and the operational
question concerns the ignition class. For FSR, RMSE and \(R^2\) are two
legitimate ranking axes that largely agree, while the residual plot shows
that pooled scores hide underprediction of the high-spread tail. The
defensible result is a screening tool with explicit domain and uncertainty
limits, not a universal flammability law.
