\section{Ignition classification}
\label{sec:results-ignition}

\subsection{ROC, precision--recall, and the fire-safety operating question}
\label{sec:ignition-auc}
The ignition metrics and threshold-selection procedure are defined in
\cref{sec:classification-method}. This section reports the held-out ROC and
precision--recall behaviour of the selected XGBoost classifier and interprets
their implications for a fire-safety screening use case.

ROC--AUC summarizes whether the classifier ranks ignition observations above
no-ignition observations across all possible thresholds. PR--AUC is especially
important here because ignition is the majority label: it measures whether
positive predictions remain reliable above the prevalence baseline. A fixed
decision threshold is not selected from the held-out data; thresholds are
chosen inside nested training evaluation and then applied to the outer test
partition.

\Cref{fig:ignition-auc} is an illustrative example of ROC and
precision--recall curves produced by an ignition XGBoost model on one held-out
partition. It is included to explain how the two curves should be read; it is
not a summary of the selected champion or of the best model in the portfolio.

The ROC curve rises toward the upper-left corner and reaches ROC--AUC
\(=0.897\) in this example. This indicates that the model generally assigns
higher ignition probabilities to ignition observations than to no-ignition
observations. However, ROC--AUC alone does not determine whether a useful
screening threshold exists, because it does not show the number of false alarms
created by a particular threshold.

The PR curve shows the trade-off between precision and recall for the same
example. Precision remains close to one until recall reaches approximately
\(0.7\), then decreases as the threshold is lowered to identify more
ignitions. Its average precision is \(0.963\), well above the positive-class
prevalence baseline of \(0.74\). The figure therefore illustrates why PR--AUC
is important for this majority-ignition dataset: it shows whether predicted
ignitions remain credible while recall is increased.

The selected interpolation and extrapolation champions are compared separately
using repeated outer-fold results in \cref{fig:ignition-family-auc}. Their
selection is based on the frozen nested-evaluation procedure, not on the
single illustrative partition shown in \cref{fig:ignition-auc}.

\subsection{Interpolation and extrapolation by model family}

The selected interpolation classifier is the paper-weighted XGBoost recipe
\texttt{xgb\_paper\_sqrt}. Across 15 repeated stratified outer folds it
reaches ROC--AUC \(0.9076\pm0.0102\), PR--AUC \(0.9687\pm0.0039\), and mean
Brier score \(0.1063\). The selected extrapolation classifier is the
unweighted \texttt{xgb\_baseline}. Its paper-grouped ROC--AUC is
\(0.7183\pm0.0444\), PR--AUC \(0.8767\pm0.0298\), and Brier score \(0.1791\).
The paper-cluster bootstrap estimate of grouped ROC--AUC is \(0.7101\) with
95\% interval \(0.6408\)--\(0.7848\); leave-one-paper-out robustness is
similar (\(0.7020\), \(0.6376\)--\(0.7751\)).

\Cref{fig:ignition-family-auc} compares the best persisted candidate within
each model family. It does not show every candidate in the portfolio. For each
family, the candidate with the strongest eligible performance under the
corresponding protocol is plotted so that the figure compares the best
available representative of XGBoost, SVM, MLP, decision tree, and
\(k\)-nearest neighbours.

Circles represent row-level interpolation, where rows from a paper may appear
in both training and test data. Triangles represent paper-grouped
extrapolation, where complete source papers are withheld. The horizontal
distance between a circle and triangle for the same family is therefore the
loss of performance when the model is applied to an unseen experimental source.

Panel (a) reports ROC--AUC and panel (b) reports PR--AUC. In every family, the
interpolation circle lies to the right of the extrapolation triangle, showing
that performance decreases when complete papers are withheld. Extrapolation
also has larger error bars, indicating that performance depends more strongly
on which papers are held out.

XGBoost is the strongest family on ROC--AUC in both protocols. For PR--AUC,
XGBoost is strongest under interpolation, whereas the extrapolated SVM and
XGBoost representatives are close. The important result is not only which
family ranks first, but that every family loses performance when the evaluation
changes from within-source interpolation to transfer across an unseen paper.

Probability ranking and probability calibration are different properties.
\Cref{fig:ignition-calibration} compares the probability predicted by each
champion with the fraction of observations that actually ignite. A perfectly
calibrated model would follow the dashed diagonal: for example, observations
predicted at probability \(0.8\) would ignite approximately \(80\%\) of the
time.

The interpolation champion follows the diagonal reasonably closely. Its
probabilities can therefore be interpreted as useful estimates within the
experimental sources represented in training.

The extrapolation champion does not follow the diagonal as closely. At high
predicted probabilities, the observed ignition fraction is lower than the
predicted probability. In simple terms, when the model is applied to an unseen
paper, it can be too confident that ignition will occur. Its probabilities
remain useful for ranking cases, but they should not be interpreted directly
as calibrated ignition risks without additional paper-aware calibration.

interpolation champion follows the diagonal reasonably closely. The
extrapolation champion lies below the diagonal at high predicted probabilities,
showing that it can overestimate ignition probability on held-out papers.}
  \label{fig:ignition-calibration}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Ignition explainability}

The held-paper XGBoost SHAP plot in \cref{fig:ignition-explainability} shows
both contribution direction and feature value. Oxygen has the largest global
SHAP magnitude, followed by absolute flow speed, Prandtl number, gas molar
mass, fuel pyrolysis temperature, and ignition time. Higher oxygen values
generally move predictions toward ignition in this fitted model. Low absolute
flow speed produces large negative SHAP values, a pattern consistent with a
quenching branch in which weak transport cannot sustain a flame. Low fuel
pyrolysis temperatures also tend to push the model toward ignition. These
patterns show that the fitted model represents nonlinear associations, but they
do not prove that it has recovered the underlying combustion mechanisms.

Permutation importance gives a different ranking: fuel pyrolysis temperature,
ignition time, fuel heat capacity, and thermal diffusion time produce the
largest positive held-out ROC--AUC losses when shuffled. Oxygen has high SHAP
magnitude but is not the leading permutation feature in this held-out sample.
The disagreement is evidence of correlated and substitutable predictors, not a
contradiction and not a causal ranking. Oxygen, flow, and thermal time scales
nonetheless recur across both views, which is the level of interpretation the
observational table can support.

\section{Flame-spread-rate regression}
\label{sec:results-fsr}


\subsection{Interpolation and extrapolation}

The regression metrics are defined in \cref{sec:regression-method}. RMSE is the
primary physical-unit error metric, MAE describes typical absolute error, and
\(R^2\) measures performance relative to a mean predictor.

Each FSR candidate is evaluated using raw training data \texttt{rd} and raw
training data plus bootstrap-resampled copies \texttt{rd\_bt}. The copies are
drawn only from the current outer-training partition; outer-test rows are never
used. As illustrated in
\cref{fig:app-fsr-bootstrap-distribution}, augmentation changes the frequency
of existing observations during fitting but does not create new FSR values,
materials, rigs, or physical regimes.

Interpolation evaluates new rows from source papers that may otherwise be
represented in training. Extrapolation evaluates transfer to entirely unseen
source papers.

The selected model for both protocols is the raw-data random-forest baseline,
\texttt{rf\_baseline [rd]}. Across ten repeated row-level holdouts, it reaches
\(R^2=0.8790\pm0.0178\),
RMSE \(=0.006263\pm0.000472~\si{\metre\per\second}\), and
MAE \(=0.002103\pm0.000173~\si{\metre\per\second}\). Across ten
paper-grouped holdouts, it reaches \(R^2=0.4576\pm0.2215\),
RMSE \(=0.012205\pm0.004115~\si{\metre\per\second}\), and
MAE \(=0.004819\pm0.001144~\si{\metre\per\second}\).

The augmentation condition does not provide a consistent improvement. For
example, augmented XGBoost reaches an interpolation RMSE of
\(0.006236~\si{\metre\per\second}\), but this is within the declared
\(2\%\) tie band and has greater fold-to-fold variability than the selected
raw-data random forest. Repeating existing rows therefore does not address the
main challenge: transfer to an unseen experimental source.

\Cref{fig:fsr-model-comparison} compares every persisted candidate under both
evaluation protocols and both training conditions. Blue bars represent
row-level interpolation and orange bars represent paper-grouped extrapolation.
For each candidate, the raw-data and augmentation bars are generally close
together. Their difference is small compared with the substantially larger
difference between interpolation and extrapolation. Tree ensembles occupy the
low-RMSE group, KNN occupies an intermediate group, and SVR and MLP candidates
occupy the higher-error group.

\subsection{Held-out parity and error structure}

The two panels of \cref{fig:fsr-parity-error} diagnose where the grouped
random forest succeeds and where it fails. Panel (a) is the extrapolation
parity plot: predicted versus experimental FSR, with the one-to-one line as
the reference. The pooled snapshot on that figure is \(R^2=0.425\) and
RMSE \(=0.0129~\si{\metre\per\second}\), consistent with the repeated-holdout
mean \(R^2=0.458\pm0.222\). Dense low-FSR observations lie near the diagonal.
Above about \(0.05~\si{\metre\per\second}\) the cloud peels away below the
line: experimental values continue to \(0.21~\si{\metre\per\second}\) while
predictions rarely exceed \(0.08~\si{\metre\per\second}\). The model
regresses toward the densely sampled low-spread mean.

Panel (b) plots the same predictions as error (predicted minus experimental)
against true FSR. At near-zero spread the errors are small and slightly
positive: the model overpredicts the slowest flames. As experimental FSR
increases, the error trend is a descending line that reaches almost
\(-0.20~\si{\metre\per\second}\) at the fastest observations, meaning a
high-spread event is predicted near zero. Error variance also widens with
true FSR (heteroscedasticity). For a fire-safety regressor this pattern is
the opposite of a conservative bias: the dangerous tail is underpredicted.
The hardest held-out paper reaches RMSE
\(0.1331~\si{\metre\per\second}\), whereas many papers remain below
\(0.003~\si{\metre\per\second}\). A single pooled \(R^2\) therefore conceals
campaign-level failure modes that the error plot makes visible.

\subsection{FSR explainability}

The FSR random forest relies most strongly on thermal diffusion time, oxygen,
gas thermal conductivity, and flow velocity in its SHAP summary
(\cref{fig:fsr-explainability}). The beeswarm adds direction: high oxygen
generally pushes FSR upward, while large thermal diffusion times---a thick or
thermally inertial solid---push FSR downward. Held-out permutation importance
independently identifies oxygen, flow velocity, gas conductivity, and thermal
diffusion time as the largest positive RMSE degradations.

Several correlated material variables have negative permutation importance in
the realized held-out sample. That means shuffling happened to reduce error;
it does not imply a physically protective effect. The repeated appearance of
oxygen, flow, and thermal transport across two explanation methods supports
their use as hypotheses and coverage priorities, not as causal coefficients.

\section{Synthesis}

The figures establish one common result across two targets and different model
families: within-paper interpolation is strong, while transfer to an unseen
paper is moderate and substantially more uncertain. Ignition ROC--AUC falls
from approximately \(0.91\) to \(0.72\), and PR--AUC from \(0.97\) to
\(0.88\); FSR RMSE approximately doubles and \(R^2\) falls from \(0.879\) to
\(0.458\). Family identity is stable: tree ensembles lead both tasks on both
protocols. These protocol effects are larger than the differences among the
leading tree recipes.

PR--AUC complements ROC--AUC for ignition screening because it reports the
precision cost of increasing ignition recall against the majority-positive
prevalence baseline. For FSR, RMSE and \(R^2\) are two
legitimate ranking axes that largely agree, while the residual plot shows
that pooled scores hide underprediction of the high-spread tail. The
defensible result is a screening tool with explicit domain and uncertainty
limits, not a universal flammability law.
