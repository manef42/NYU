\chapter{Results}
\label{chap:results}

\section{Data integrity and scope of inference}
\label{sec:results-integrity}

Both persisted evaluation suites passed their overall integrity checks.  The
ignition analysis used 4,487 deduplicated rows from 87 canonical papers, with 3,383
positive and 1,104 negative labels.  Its frozen split manifest recorded no
integrity failures across 120 split objects.  The FSR analysis used 2,531 rows with
numeric FSR from 66 papers, and all 20 repeated holdout partitions passed their
coverage and separation checks.  The corresponding SHA-256 fingerprints were
\texttt{d80da0648ca3a7d77d31ae90ebea53b4e81b2ab28c8ec666ce6cd150ee7544f6}
for ignition and
\texttt{6e09a99e4ea69148e69151bd46149be4140d181b2b8294106927ea9499002fc8}
for FSR.  These fingerprints establish which persisted data versions the numerical
results describe.

The principal result throughout this chapter is the difference between row-level
interpolation and paper-grouped extrapolation.  The former permits a publication
to contribute observations to both sides of a split and is consequently a measure
of prediction among represented campaigns.  The latter excludes every test
paper from training and is the relevant test of transfer to new literature
campaigns.  Neither protocol evaluates causal mechanisms, and neither extends the
claim beyond the support of the compiled database.

\section{Ignition classification}
\label{sec:results-ignition}

\subsection{Model selection and interpolation performance}

The selected interpolation model was \texttt{xgb\_paper\_sqrt}, an XGBoost
classifier with paper weight \(1/\sqrt{n_p}\) and no stated class-weight addition.
Across the 15 repeated stratified outer folds, its ROC--AUC was
\(0.9076\pm0.0102\), PR--AUC was \(0.9687\pm0.0039\), and mean Brier score was
0.1063.  Its modal refit configuration used 800 trees, maximum depth 6, learning
rate 0.05, column subsampling 0.6, row subsampling 1.0, minimum child weight 1,
\(\gamma=0\), \(L_1\) regularization 0.5, \(L_2\) regularization 1.0, and the
logistic objective.  The supplementary three-repeat 80/20 holdout was consistent:
ROC--AUC \(0.9032\pm0.0016\), PR--AUC \(0.9663\pm0.0015\), and Brier score 0.1114.

The leading XGBoost variants were tightly clustered in row interpolation
(Table~\ref{tab:ignition-leaders}).  The unweighted XGBoost baseline achieved
\(0.9058\pm0.0091\) ROC--AUC, only 0.0018 below the selected model.  The
class-weighted and combined square-root-weighted variants were similarly close.
By contrast, the leading reported SVM reached 0.8608, the leading decision tree
0.8531, and the leading KNN 0.8325.  The two reported focal-objective XGBoost
recipes were near chance in ROC ranking (0.4960 and 0.4987), despite high PR--AUC
values caused in part by the 75.4\% positive prevalence.  This combination
illustrates why PR--AUC must be interpreted against prevalence and why candidate
selection did not rely on it alone.

The row-resampled bootstrap gives a complementary uncertainty summary.  After
averaging repeated probabilities for each unique row, the interpolation champion
had ROC--AUC 0.9122 (95\% CI 0.9037--0.9209), PR--AUC 0.9703
(0.9660--0.9741), and Brier score 0.1023 (0.0969--0.1078).  These bootstrap
estimates need not equal the arithmetic means of the 15 fold metrics because they
aggregate repeated predictions per row before computing each metric.

\subsection{Paper-grouped extrapolation and the cross-paper gap}

The extrapolation champion was the unweighted \texttt{xgb\_baseline}.  Its grouped
outer-fold ROC--AUC was \(0.7183\pm0.0444\), PR--AUC
\(0.8767\pm0.0298\), and Brier score 0.1791.  The selected modal configuration was
substantially more regularized and shallower than the interpolation champion: 400
trees, maximum depth 2, learning rate 0.01, column subsampling 0.6, row subsampling
0.6, minimum child weight 4, \(\gamma=0.1\), \(L_1=0.5\), \(L_2=1.0\), and the
logistic objective.  The mandatory LOPO analysis was successful.  Its
paper-cluster bootstrap ROC--AUC was 0.7020 (95\% CI 0.6376--0.7751), PR--AUC
0.8622 (0.8013--0.9222), and Brier score 0.1734 (0.1293--0.2112).

For the primary grouped protocol, the same paper-cluster procedure yielded
ROC--AUC 0.7101 (95\% CI 0.6408--0.7848), PR--AUC 0.8683
(0.8110--0.9277), and Brier score 0.1713 (0.1234--0.2124).  These intervals are
considerably wider than the interpolation intervals, reflecting both fewer
independent paper units and heterogeneity among campaigns.

The unweighted baseline's ROC--AUC fell from 0.9058 under stratified row
interpolation to 0.7183 under grouped extrapolation, an absolute reduction of
0.1875.  For the interpolation champion itself, the corresponding reduction was
larger: 0.9076 to 0.7004, or 0.2072.  Combined class and paper weighting did not
remove the gap; the square-root-weighted XGBoost model dropped by 0.2215.  The
decision-tree baseline had the largest reported reduction, 0.2414.  Linear SVM
variants showed smaller numerical drops of 0.0746--0.0855, but this was because
their interpolation scores were already lower, not because their grouped scores
were superior.  The focal variants' near-zero gaps likewise reflect near-chance
performance in both settings and are not evidence of robust transfer.

This deterioration is the clearest classification result.  Rows within represented
papers can be ranked with high accuracy, but transfer to unseen papers is only
moderate and more uncertain.  The change in selected candidate reinforces the
point: square-root paper weighting narrowly led within-paper interpolation, whereas
the plain baseline generalized best under the declared grouped policy.  A
single ``best ignition model'' without its evaluation protocol would therefore be
misleading.

\subsection{Operational thresholds}

Threshold choice produced materially different operating characteristics
(Table~\ref{tab:ignition-thresholds}).  For the interpolation champion, the
mean MCC threshold was 0.7920, giving sensitivity 0.7916, specificity 0.8723, and
precision 0.9500 over persisted outer predictions.  The F1-optimal threshold was
much lower at 0.3688; sensitivity increased to 0.9516 while specificity fell to
0.4798.  Balanced accuracy and Youden's \(J\) selected the same mean threshold,
0.8379, with sensitivity 0.7660 and specificity 0.9013.

For the extrapolation champion, the MCC threshold of 0.8099 produced sensitivity
0.7290 and specificity 0.5836.  The F1 threshold, 0.2850, classified nearly every
row as positive: sensitivity was 0.9837 but specificity only 0.0592.  Balanced
accuracy and Youden's \(J\) again coincided, at threshold 0.8583, balancing
sensitivity 0.6639 and specificity 0.6621.  This sensitivity--specificity exchange
is especially consequential in an imbalanced data set.  The persisted results
support retaining all four policies for application-specific decisions rather
than presenting one threshold as universally preferable.

\subsection{Ignition model reliance}

SHAP and permutation rankings presented complementary descriptions of the
XGBoost models.  In the interpolation champion, the largest mean absolute SHAP
magnitudes were oxygen (0.6150), Prandtl number (0.4154), absolute flow speed
(0.3429), gas molar mass (0.3140), ignition time (0.2521), fuel pyrolysis
temperature (0.2241), and the third sample dimension (0.2140).  In the
extrapolation champion, oxygen remained first (0.3591), followed by absolute flow
speed (0.2500), Prandtl number (0.2497), gas molar mass (0.2047), fuel pyrolysis
temperature (0.2044), ignition time (0.1638), and signed flow velocity (0.1271).
These are average absolute contribution magnitudes in the fitted score space; they
do not indicate whether larger values increase or decrease ignition probability.

Held-out permutation importance gave a different ordering.  For the extrapolation
champion, whose unpermuted held-out ROC--AUC in this analysis was 0.7818, the
largest mean losses were obtained for fuel pyrolysis temperature
\(0.0977\pm0.0051\), ignition time \(0.0452\pm0.0066\), fuel specific heat
\(0.0279\pm0.0019\), thermal diffusion time \(0.0206\pm0.0020\), the third sample
dimension \(0.0158\pm0.0021\), and absolute flow speed
\(0.0130\pm0.0085\).  Oxygen, despite leading SHAP magnitude, had permutation
importance \(-0.0054\pm0.0085\).  Likewise, Prandtl number was
\(-0.0200\pm0.0067\).  This disagreement is expected when predictors are
correlated or substitutable: a model can allocate substantial local contributions
to a variable even if shuffling that variable does not degrade aggregate ranking
because related variables preserve information.  It would be incorrect to
translate either ranking into a causal hierarchy of combustion controls.

\section{Flame-spread-rate regression}
\label{sec:results-fsr}

\subsection{Interpolation model comparison}

The declared selection policy chose the unaugmented random-forest baseline
\texttt{rf\_baseline} for row interpolation.  Across ten repeated row holdouts it
achieved RMSE \(0.006263\pm0.000472~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), MAE
\(0.002103\pm0.000173~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), and
\(R^2=0.8790\pm0.0178\).  Its mean bias error was
\(-3.88\times10^{-5}~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).  The modal configuration used 500
trees, maximum depth 10, 0.8 of the available features at each split, minimum leaf
size 1, and minimum split size 2.

The numerically lowest interpolation RMSE belonged to bootstrap-augmented
\texttt{xgb\_baseline}, \(0.006236\pm0.000698~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), but it lay
within the 2\% tie band and had \(R^2=0.8783\pm0.0310\).  The unaugmented random
forest was selected after the higher-\(R^2\), lower-uncertainty, and
no-augmentation tie-breakers.  Other competitive results included unaugmented
\texttt{xgb\_absoluteerror\_sqrt}, with RMSE
\(0.006338\pm0.000680~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), MAE
\(0.002028\pm0.000166~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), and \(R^2=0.8752\pm0.0294\).
KNN, MLP, and SVR candidates were materially weaker in the leading persisted
rankings: the best listed KNN condition had RMSE 0.0090, the best listed MLP 0.0102,
and the best listed SVR 0.0106 \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).

The row bootstrap for the selected random forest, after averaging repeated
predictions per row, estimated RMSE \(0.005955~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) (95\% CI
0.004734--0.007146), MAE \(0.001973~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
(0.001759--0.002215), and \(R^2=0.8845\) (0.8449--0.9198).  As for
classification, these pooled-prediction estimates differ slightly from the mean
of foldwise metrics because the order of aggregation and metric calculation is
different.

\subsection{Unseen-paper performance and uncertainty}

The same unaugmented random-forest candidate was selected for paper extrapolation,
but with a distinct modal configuration: 500 trees, maximum depth 12, feature
fraction 0.5, minimum leaf size 8, and minimum split size 2.  Its ten grouped
holdouts yielded RMSE \(0.012205\pm0.004115~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), MAE
\(0.004819\pm0.001144~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), and
\(R^2=0.4576\pm0.2215\).  Mean bias was
\(-0.000658~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).  Fold-level ranges emphasize instability across
paper composition: RMSE varied from 0.007775 to
\(0.022121~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), while \(R^2\) ranged from 0.0176 to 0.7465.

Paper-cluster bootstrap intervals were correspondingly broad.  The grouped
random-forest estimate was RMSE \(0.011867~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) (95\% CI
0.006525--0.017839), MAE \(0.004482~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
(0.002889--0.006528), and \(R^2=0.4893\) (0.2773--0.7319).  The interval width
is part of the scientific result: transfer accuracy depends strongly on which
papers are withheld, and the effective extrapolation sample comprises papers,
not thousands of exchangeable rows.

The nearest grouped competitors did not clearly overturn the random-forest
selection.  Augmented \texttt{rf\_baseline} had RMSE
\(0.012218\pm0.004060~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) and
\(R^2=0.4477\pm0.2468\).  Augmented \texttt{xgb\_paper\_sqrt} had RMSE
\(0.012572\pm0.003832~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) and
\(R^2=0.4411\pm0.1548\).  Augmented
\texttt{xgb\_absoluteerror\_inverse} had RMSE
\(0.012632\pm0.003872~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) and
\(R^2=0.4223\pm0.1973\).  Six candidate--condition combinations were rejected by
the extrapolation policy: both data conditions for
\texttt{mlp\_log\_target\_sqrt}, \texttt{mlp\_log\_target\_inverse}, and
\texttt{svr\_linear}.  Their mean grouped \(R^2\) values ranged from \(-0.696\)
to \(-3.093\), and several also exceeded the RMSE coefficient-of-variation limit.

\subsection{FSR cross-paper gap and augmentation}

For the selected random forest, grouped RMSE exceeded row-interpolation RMSE by
\(0.005942\pm0.004138~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), almost doubling from 0.006263 to
\(0.012205~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).  Its \(R^2\) fell from 0.8790 to 0.4576, a
reduction of 0.4214.  MAE similarly increased from 0.002103 to
\(0.004819~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).  The gap is not attributable to a change of
metric or model family: it appears for the same candidate evaluated under the two
split mechanisms.  It therefore quantifies the optimism that would result from
describing random-row scores as new-paper performance.

Bootstrap augmentation did not provide a systematic advantage.  For the selected
random forest, augmentation changed interpolation RMSE by
\(+0.0000265\pm0.0001985~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) and grouped RMSE by
\(+0.0000131\pm0.0004498~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), where positive values favor the
unaugmented condition.  Some recipes improved: grouped
\texttt{xgb\_paper\_sqrt} changed by
\(-0.000392\pm0.000690~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), and
\texttt{xgb\_log\_target\_inverse} by
\(-0.000665\pm0.001507~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).  Others worsened, including grouped
\texttt{xgb\_baseline} by \(+0.000554\pm0.000615~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).
The magnitudes were generally small relative to between-fold uncertainty.  The
selection of \texttt{rd} for both champions is thus consistent with the empirical
results and the policy preference for real data within the tie band.  It does not
imply that bootstrap copies were invalid; it shows that resampling observed
training rows did not robustly solve transfer to unseen papers.

\subsection{Paper-level heterogeneity}

Aggregated performance concealed severe paper-specific errors.  For the grouped
random-forest champion, the hardest reported paper was
\texttt{doi::10.1016/j.combustflame.2020.12.005}, with RMSE
\(0.13313~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), MAE \(0.12536~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), and
\(R^2=-13.35\).  The next paper had RMSE \(0.07729~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).
Several others had RMSE near \(0.02~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), while many papers were
predicted with errors below \(0.003~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).  Extremely negative
per-paper \(R^2\) values also occurred for papers with little within-paper target
variance; in that setting, a modest absolute error can be large relative to the
paper-specific variance denominator.  Accordingly, per-paper RMSE and MAE are more
stable indicators of absolute predictive error than isolated per-paper \(R^2\).
The heterogeneous error distribution is consistent with the wide grouped fold SD
and bootstrap intervals and argues against a universal accuracy claim.

\subsection{FSR model reliance}

The random-forest champion's impurity-based importance placed flow velocity first
(0.2187), followed by thermal diffusion time (0.1830), oxygen concentration
(0.1013), characteristic length (0.0830), the parsed sample-dimension field
(0.0698), gas thermal conductivity (0.0658), Péclet number (0.0476), and Reynolds
number (0.0402).  Impurity importance is included as a model summary but is known
to favor variables that offer many potential split points and does not measure
held-out dependence.

Mean absolute SHAP magnitude gave thermal diffusion time the largest value,
\(0.005293~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), followed by oxygen concentration
\(0.003105\), gas thermal conductivity \(0.001441\), flow velocity \(0.001089\),
fuel volumetric heat capacity \(0.001034\), fuel density \(0.000949\), core
volumetric heat capacity \(0.000852\), and core density
\(0.000787~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).  These magnitudes summarize how strongly features
contributed to predictions in the evaluated artifact; taking absolute values
removes direction.

Permutation importance was computed on a held-out set whose unpermuted RMSE was
\(0.010919~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).  The clearest positive degradations were oxygen
concentration, \(0.002276\pm0.000349~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\); flow velocity,
\(0.001486\pm0.000782\); gas thermal conductivity,
\(0.001137\pm0.000083\); and thermal diffusion time,
\(0.000967\pm0.000682~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).  Prandtl number, gas specific heat,
and Reynolds number had smaller positive values of \(0.000271\), \(0.000156\),
and \(0.000154~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), respectively.  Several correlated
thermophysical variables had negative permutation values, including fuel
volumetric heat capacity (\(-0.000837\)), fuel density (\(-0.000790\)), and core
volumetric heat capacity (\(-0.000665~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)).  These negative
values indicate that permutation happened to reduce RMSE in this held-out sample;
they do not establish an inverse physical effect.

Across the three summaries, oxygen concentration, flow, gas conductivity, and
thermal diffusion time repeatedly appeared near the top, which is evidence that
the fitted predictor relied on information associated with those variables.
Nevertheless, the database contains strongly related raw and derived transport
quantities, campaign-specific missingness, and uneven coverage of materials and
conditions.  Importance can therefore migrate among correlated predictors, and
none of the rankings identifies a causal intervention.  In particular, the results
do not demonstrate that changing one variable while holding all else fixed would
produce the corresponding change in FSR.

\section{Synthesis}
\label{sec:results-synthesis}

The two tasks exhibit the same structural result despite different targets,
cohorts, models, and metrics.  Ignition ROC--AUC declined from approximately 0.91
under row interpolation to 0.72 for the grouped champion.  FSR RMSE approximately
doubled and \(R^2\) fell from 0.879 to 0.458 when complete papers were withheld.
Uncertainty also expanded under grouped resampling.  These gaps are direct
evidence that campaign membership captures dependencies not eliminated by the
standardized predictor set.

The selected families were tree ensembles in all cases, but the configurations
and weighting choices differed by prediction question.  Paper weighting narrowly
won ignition interpolation yet not ignition extrapolation.  The random-forest
baseline won both FSR protocols, with a deeper but much larger minimum leaf size
for grouped transfer.  Bootstrap augmentation changed some regression candidates
in either direction but produced no general reduction in cross-paper error.
Threshold optimization substantially changed ignition sensitivity and specificity,
so deployment requires an explicit operating objective.

These findings support a bounded conclusion.  The database contains enough
repeated structure for strong within-campaign prediction and enough transferable
signal for performance above naive baselines on average across unseen papers.
However, the cross-paper penalties, wide grouped uncertainty, and extreme
paper-level failures preclude universal claims.  SHAP and permutation analyses
identify predictive reliance, not causal combustion laws.  The defensible outputs
are therefore protocol-specific estimates with their uncertainty and documented
feature context, rather than a single headline score or a mechanistic claim.
