\section{Spacecraft fire safety}

Fire safety is a system-level requirement for crewed spacecraft. A pressurized
vehicle necessarily combines oxidizer, combustible materials, and potential
ignition sources in a confined volume where evacuation, replacement of damaged
equipment, and atmospheric clean-up may be impossible. Electrical systems are
indispensable to vehicle operation, yet faults, overheated components, and
degraded insulation place ignition energy next to polymeric fuels. Material
selection is consequently a compromise among fire performance, function, mass,
manufacturability, and mission architecture rather than a simple elimination of
all combustible items \cite{guibaud2022spacecraft}.

Apollo~1 remains the clearest demonstration of coupled fire hazards. During a
ground test on 27 January 1967, a fire developed rapidly in the command module's
high-pressure pure-oxygen atmosphere. The exact ignition source was not
established by the investigation, but electrical wiring was considered a likely
source; extensive combustible material, the atmosphere, and the inward-opening
hatch made the event catastrophic. The post-fire views in
\cref{fig:apollo1} show why specimen flammability cannot be separated from
installation and vehicle configuration. The accident led to atmospheric,
materials, wiring, hatch, and test-program changes, including extensive
component and full-scale mock-up testing \cite{guibaud2022spacecraft}.

Deep-space missions increase the consequences of such an event. A distant
vehicle or surface habitat cannot assume rapid evacuation, immediate resupply,
or prompt replacement of discharged extinguishers and damaged life-support
hardware. Proposed low-pressure atmospheres, elevated oxygen fraction, lunar or
Martian gravity, radiation aging, dust, waste accumulation, and long autonomous
operation all interact with fire risk \cite{guibaud2022spacecraft}. A material
qualified in one terrestrial configuration therefore cannot be assumed to keep
the same hazard ranking in every spacecraft environment.

\section{Flammability, quenching, and blowoff}
\label{sec:intro-flammability}

In this report, \emph{flammability} is not reduced to a single binary property.
It is represented by five complementary experimental observables:

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{Five variables used to describe material flammability in the database.}
  \label{tab:flammability-variables}
  \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}p{0.19\textwidth}X@{}}
    \toprule
    Variable & Engineering interpretation \\
    \midrule
    Ignition & Whether the imposed energy and environment establish a flame. \\
    Flame length & Spatial extent of the visible flame under the test condition. \\
    Flame-spread rate (FSR) & Speed of the advancing flame front,
      reported in \si{\metre\per\second}. \\
    Heat-release rate (HRR) & Rate of chemical energy release,
      reported in \si{\watt} when available. \\
    Smoke/aerosols & Production of particulate and condensable combustion
      products relevant to visibility, toxicity, and detection. \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabularx}
\end{table}

These variables describe different stages and consequences of a fire. Ignition
addresses initiation; flame length, FSR, and HRR characterize growth; smoke and
aerosols affect detection and habitability. Their availability is uneven across
the literature. The implemented machine-learning pipelines model only ignition
and FSR. Flame length, HRR, and smoke/aerosols are retained as database outputs
but excluded from predictors to prevent outcome leakage.

The stability of an established flame is bounded by two distinct extinction
mechanisms. At high imposed flow or stretch, the gas residence time through the
reaction zone becomes too short relative to the chemical reaction time. The
flame detaches or is extinguished by \emph{blowoff}, conventionally associated
with a low Damköhler number. Self-induced buoyant flow can also produce blowoff
during upward spread in normal gravity \cite{johnston2015blowoff}.

At the opposite limit, very weak flow supplies insufficient convective heat
feedback and oxygen transport to offset radiative and conductive losses. The
flame shrinks and extinguishes by \emph{quenching}. The low-speed quenching
branch is experimentally accessible in microgravity because buoyancy no longer
imposes a minimum natural-convection velocity. In ordinary gravity, buoyant flow
masks this near-zero-flow branch; this is the precise sense in which the
quenching limit considered here is observed only under microgravity conditions,
not a claim that every possible form of flame quenching is exclusive to space.

Plotting limiting oxygen concentration against forced stretch or flow gives the
U-shaped boundary in \cref{fig:flammability-map}. The left branch is the
microgravity low-flow quenching limit, the right branch is the high-flow
blowoff limit, and stable burning occupies the region above the boundary. The
minimum identifies the lowest oxygen concentration at which any tested flow can
sustain combustion. This map demonstrates why oxygen concentration alone is
insufficient: the same material and atmosphere may be non-flammable at very low
and very high flow yet burn at an intermediate flow.

\section{Research gap and data-driven motivation}

Reduced gravity changes the balance among diffusion, imposed ventilation,
radiation, and solid conduction. Experiments are correspondingly expensive and
the published evidence is fragmented across materials, geometries, facilities,
atmospheres, and reporting conventions. A finite qualification matrix cannot
dense-sample this space, while a high-fidelity model requires boundary
conditions and material properties that are often absent from historical
reports.

The research gap is therefore not simply a lack of another predictive
algorithm. It is the absence of a unified, traceable, leakage-resistant
evaluation that asks both whether models interpolate among conditions from
known campaigns and whether they transfer to a completely held-out paper.
Randomly splitting rows can place nearly repeated observations from one rig on
both sides of the evaluation boundary, creating optimistic evidence for a
material-selection question that usually concerns a new campaign.

This project addresses that gap by standardizing published measurements into a
microgravity flammability database and constructing separate supervised
pipelines for ignition classification and FSR regression. The intended role is
screening and experiment prioritization, not replacement of qualification tests
or combustion physics.

\section{Research question and objectives}

\begin{quote}
\emph{To what extent can supervised models trained on a curated, multi-source
microgravity-combustion database predict (i) whether ignition occurs and (ii)
the flame-spread rate under spacecraft-relevant material, geometric,
atmospheric, flow, and gravity conditions, while maintaining performance when
entire experimental papers are withheld?}
\end{quote}

The work has four objectives:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item assemble a source-traceable database with consistent variables and SI
  units from heterogeneous publications;
  \item construct clear, reproducible classification and regression pipelines;
  \item compare row-level interpolation with paper-grouped extrapolation; and
  \item interpret model behavior using held-out diagnostics, permutation
  importance, and SHAP without treating predictive associations as causal laws.
\end{enumerate}

Only ignition and FSR are modeled in the final repository. Extinction,
flame length, HRR, and smoke/aerosol prediction remain future modeling tasks.
This boundary preserves a clear distinction between the database's definition
of flammability and the narrower scope of the validated machine-learning
evidence.
